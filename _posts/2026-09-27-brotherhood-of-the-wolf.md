Marquis d'Apcher writes his memoirs during the French Revolution, the people outside ready to tear down the door. We step into his account, watching a woman, bloodied and bruised, running from the sound of growling through the French mountainside. The growling catches up and attacks

Later, a group of riotous men beat up a woman in the rain for being a witch. Two masked men on horseback step in and fight the rioters using martial arts, and question the rioters' motives. The men on horseback let the woman and her father leave in peace. One man is Sir Grégoire de Fronsac, a knight of King Louis XV—the other, his mysterious Iroquois companion, Mani.

These men are in Gévaudan to hunt the beast, carrying their own collection of drawings and specimens of oddities. They consult with a Marquis who has hunted the beast. He also tends to those the beast has wounded with a fully-stocked infirmary. The men interview the survivors for descriptions, and hear of an attack. They find the victim's body, her torso torn open. Around her lay traps, and her body was filled with poison, in case the beast returns.

Sir de Fronsac takes an interest in Marianne de Morangias, the count's daughter. He also meets Jean-François, Mme. de Morangias' brother, who also hunts. At a dinner with dukes and royalty, he shows off a faux furry fish to make a point about the separation between the mythical and the scientific. The king offers a reward for the beast.

And so the hunt begins.

<blockquote><i>Welcome to the lands of the beasts, my lords.</i></blockquote>

The film is a mishmash of genre tropes, blending (but not limited to) horror, martial arts, historical drama, and mystery. The film makes no effort to convey all this with a consistent visual language. The drama is lushly colored and static. The fights feel like wuxia, but like 1995 <i>Mortal Kombat'</i>s notion of wuxia. The horror is straight-up <i>Evil Dead</i>, with frenetic cameras and cartoonish violence.

With such sprawling ambition, the movie seems to lack any internal drive or momentum beyond "the hunt." We spend a fair bit of time in a brothel, where Monica Bellucci spouts vaguely witchy omens and portents. We also get the longest taxidermy scene.

The film uses historical events (very loosely) to explore the corruption of French rule. Starting with the French Revolution and working back, we see that the king wasn't interested in solving the state's problems, but was always looking to save face. By contrasting the white elite's hunting methods versus Mani's, we see how much empty posturing and performance lives in the wealthy. That said, I don't think the movie has a clear idea of what it wants to say, especially as these concepts muddy over the course of the movie.

The film wants to make everyone horny, but it's all for the male gaze, whether it realizes it or not. The lead is such a male power fantasy that it's hard to root for him. Mani does not get much in the way of character, but serves multiple aesthetic purposes. The actor can do the fight stunts, and the movie goes out of its way to showcase many shirtless scenes. Did I mention we spend a lot of time in a brothel?

The film is French, so it goes without saying that its depictions of race are problematic and deeply flawed. The film has a, I don't know, hunting cult, and it's clear the movie wants us to compare it to the Romani. Also, I keep bringing up Mani, but he is a pile of stereotypes, including a scene where he tells all the white people their spirit animals as a party trick.

Still, for all its meandering and flaws, I never really lost interest. In fact, I kind of dig the overall vibes. It doesn't all work, but I appreciate the swing, and I don't mind cheesy, dated CGI. Maybe my slightly higher rating is due to prolonged exposure to the director's cut. Oh well!
