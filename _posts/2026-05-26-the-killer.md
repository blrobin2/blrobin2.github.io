<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 16/52 | 1980s</a></b>

<b>Slow motion, trillions of candles and birds, flashbacks to moments that happened minutes ago, and speedboat chases — we're in peak John Woo territory.</b>

Rain pours outside The Church of Salvation. Ah Jong sits in a pew, surrounded by more lit candles than stars in the sky. Fung Sei brings Ah Jong a briefcase containing weapons, money, and a photo of his target, and takes him to a nightclub called the Stardust. Gunfire interrupts nightclub singer Jennie's singing. Bodies and blood fly. Ah Jong narrowly misses several bullets but takes a couple to the back. In the crossfire, he saves Jennie, but fires a gun too close to her face, and the muzzle flash burns her eyes, partially blinding her.

Jennie learns that doctors may be able to restore her sight through a cornea transplant, but they will need to act soon, and donors are nonexistent. Though Jennie cannot see him, Ah Jong attends her performances, watching over her. One night, after a show, two men attempt to mug her. Ah Jong fights them off, then vows to help Jennie and walks her home. There, he realizes that she believes the man she saw blinded her intentionally, but does not recognize that Ah John is that man.

<blockquote><i>Actually, not everyone in this world is a bad person.</i></blockquote>

Detective Li Ying goes undercover for a weapons deal. One of the gangsters recognizes Li, and a street cop interrupts the deal, leading to a shootout that kills the street cop. Li chases the target, Wong Hung, onto a tram. Ying shoots Wong, but the gunfire causes a woman on the tram to have a heart attack and die. As a result, Li's superior, Chief Inspector Dou, demotes him and assigns him to a protection detail for a corrupt politician, Wong Tung-Yuen.

Meanwhile, Ah Jong is assigned to kill Wong Tung-Yuen, another high-ranking triad boss. He asks for $1.5 million for the job, declaring that it will be his last. With this money, he can pay for Jennie's surgery, and perhaps, his salvation. But the world of hitmen and gangsters doesn't let people leave that easily. Neither will Detective Li, who spots Ah Jong during the assassination.

<blockquote><i>[The gun is] easy to pick up but hard to put down.</i></blockquote>

The film explores the relative nature of evil. Our protagonist is a hitman, hired to kill Triad members and other criminals. Detective Li Ying is a cop and, understandably, sees Ah Jong as a cop, but finds himself in a position where he has to trust Ah Jong to reach a greater evil.

Because my point of reference for John Woo is <i>Face/Off</i>, it's easy for me to see plenty of parallels between the two movies, with the relationships at play and the imagery/motifs that recur through both. That said, despite my love for <i>Face/Off</i>, it is a bit of a cartoon compared to it. Of course, the only reason <i>Face/Off</i> so closely resembles this movie is that the screenwriters were trying to emulate John Woo and didn't expect him to actually direct their film.

It's also worth pointing out how much of Michael Mann's DNA is on display. Of course, <a href="https://boxd.it/29H6fT"><i>Thief</i></a> and <i>Manhunter</i> share a fixation on troubled men and a strong use of color to convey mood. Still, movies like <a href="https://boxd.it/4im1Ef"><i>Heat</i></a>, <a href="https://boxd.it/25TiDL"><i>Collateral</i></a>, and <a href="https://boxd.it/4i4GQF"><i>Miami Vice</i></a> explore similar themes of relationships between men and the relative nature of wrongdoing.

The editing is spitfire, creating juxtapositions almost subliminally—the film ebbs between slow moments of reflection and breakneck shootouts that are barely comprehensible.

Chow Yung Fat is perfect as the "emotive assassin," as Detective Li describes him — his eyes full of remorse, care, and attention. It's the emotional core of the film and the melodramatic elements that endear me to it more than the stylized violence.

So much 90s and 00s action is indebted to this film.
