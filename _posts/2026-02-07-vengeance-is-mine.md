<blockquote><i>Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord.</i> — Romans 12:19</blockquote>

<b>January 4, 1964</b>
A line of police cars drives through the snow up winding streets. In the back of one car, Iwao Enokizu sings to himself. He reflects on the likelihood that he will receive the death sentence and how unfair it is that his life will be cut short. A detective reminds Enokizu that he cut five lives short with his own hands. They arrive at the police station, where people line the streets. Reporters fight to photograph Enokizu — a man threatens to kill him.

<b>October 18, 1963</b>
Two women working in a garden find the body of Tanejiro Shibata, whose skull was crushed and his body stabbed. Later, police discovered Daihachi Baba, who died from blood loss due to multiple stab wounds. It appears that Enokizu killed both men on the same day within a similar timeframe.

<b>January 9, 1964</b>
The police interrogate Enozuki, showing him money he used to buy a radio, covered in Tanejiro Shibata's blood. But Enokizu is only concerned with the length of his fingernails and the falling snow. They have all the evidence they need to put Enokizu away, but the higher-ups demand a confession and a detailed account of his 78 days on the run.

The film jumps in time to various points in the story, including Enokizu's childhood, giving us a fractured, conflicting portrait of the man.

The film adapts the story of real-life serial killer Akira Nishiguchi, as depicted in Ryūzō Saki's book Vengeance is Mine. Like <i>Henry: Portrait of a Serial Killer</i>, the film is frank and direct with its portrayal of violence. The serial killer story is often a reflection of the society that breeds them — that is, they are the first victim of their story. Here, in modern Japan, we see a man caught between repression and feral outbreaks of violence. This movie delves into his past to understand what drives someone to be like this. But it's a puzzle in pieces with no box to reference — not just missing pieces, but containing those of a different puzzle altogether.

The film loses a ton of steam in the middle as it explores people surrounding Enokizu and the ways they bend morality to suit their desires. It plays into the themes of how a repressive culture breeds depravity, but it just isn't presented in an especially compelling way. That said, the film finds its way back when Enokizu becomes more central to the story. We must juxtapose the vile behavior of those around him that goes unpunished with his horrifying actions. No one is innocent, but under the law, some are more guilty than others.

Overall, I wanted to like this movie more. Similar to <i><a href="https://boxd.it/5cMjwz">The Insect Woman</a></i>, now that I've seen it in full, a revisit might make me like it more.
