<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 13/52 | 1950s</a></b>

<blockquote><i>The origin of this story goes back to medieval times, when Japan had not yet emerged from the Dark Ages, and mankind had yet to awaken as human beings. It has been retold by the people for centuries and is treasured today as one of the epic folk tales of history.</i></blockquote>

The general demands that all the people of their village fight his war and pay an increased tax on rice. The governor opposed the order. In retaliation, the general exiles the governor to Tsukushi. Their family is ashamed that the governor prioritized the peasantry over their status. The governor sends his wife, Tamaki, and children, Zushiō and Anju, to her parents' home in Iwashiro. He gives Zushiō some parting words of wisdom, a statue of the goddess of mercy.

Six years later, Tamaki travels through the woods with her children, a maidservant tailing behind with their luggage. They travel the same road that the governor took, reuniting with him. As night approaches, they look for a place to lodge. None of the inns are open, as bandits and slave dealers have been wandering the area, posing as travelers. And so, they build shelter in a field near a tree.

While camping, a priestess approaches, offering Tamaki and the children a place to stay and some hot food. The priestess also offers to find them a boatsman who can take them across the water and avoid the dangerous mountains. The following day, men with boats await them, loading Tamaki and their maidservant into the boat before casting off, leaving the children restrained by the priestess and another boatsman.

They sell the mother into prostitution in Sado, then look for someone to purchase the children. They get a tip that the wealthiest man in the land, Sansho the Bailiff, may be interested in buying them. The film follows the children into adulthood and the people they become as they grow up in this environment.

<blockquote><i>A man is not a human being without mercy. Even if you are hard on yourself, be merciful to others. Men are created equal. Everyone is entitled to their happiness.</i></blockquote>

The film is unflinching in its depiction of cruelty, but it is also not indulgent. The film understands what horrors to put on screen and what to leave to our imagination. That a film over 70 years old still has that much of an impact is a testament to the care and craft at work. I don't know how a movie looks this effortless and gorgeous. The settings and compositions are remarkable, the lighting is natural but clean, and the camera captures elegant long takes with zero indulgence.

Lest you think the film is total darkness, we see some light in Sansho's son, Taro. While still a slaver, he tries to be more humane than his unspeakably cruel father. He encourages Zushiō and Anju to survive, and they learn of their father's words of mercy. He gives the children fake names so they can hide their identity. Still, some see Sansho as the ideal bailiff and encourage Taro to be more like him.

<blockquote><i>Was what my father did a crime? Is it unlawful to love your people?</i></blockquote>

The film explores the heart of morality — that everyone is entitled to happiness. Zushiō, growing into an adult under Sansho's thumb, becomes like him, callous and distant from the pain of those around him. But when the opportunity for redemption comes, Zushiō takes it in the most selfless way he can imagine. So much of what limits the happiness of others is the power that others have over them, and the lack of means to escape it. And so, the film calls into question the role of power in governance without completely rejecting it.

The film also explores memory and the transience of life. The only constant is change — whatever power you have today could be taken from you tomorrow. Whatever hardships you are enduring now could lift in ways you cannot know yet. We only experience time backward, accruing memories of the past minute, hour, day, year.  And yet, we must act. Even if no one understands our actions and choices, if we make them in full renunciation of power and ego, we will find a moral path forward.

I haven't seen a movie that so fully embodies a parable while still maintaining cinematic integrity. I also don't know if I've ever seen a tragedy inspire so much hope.
