<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 23/52 | Comedy</a></b>

After videotaping a wedding with her friend Tamara, Cheryl sets up the video camera in her apartment. She presents herself as a videographer and video store worker. Cheryl has been borrowing movies from the '30s and '40s from her video store. In most of the credits, the Black actors are not even credited. In one film, Plantation Memories, Elsie, the mammy character, was portrayed by a Black woman only credited as "The Watermelon Woman." Cheryl decides to make a film about this woman, discovering her real name and everything she can learn about her.

At work, Tamara believes that Cheryl is doing this filmmaking project just because she doesn't have a girlfriend, like Tamara and her girlfriend Stacey. So, Cheryl reluctantly goes with them to karaoke night at a lesbian bar. Stacey brings Yvette, a friend from out of town. For the film, Cheryl interviews pedestrians, asking if they know of "The Watermelon Woman." Then, she travels to Wynnfield to interview her mother, who believes she saw the woman perform on stage in a club.

The film follows Cheryl as she navigates dating as a Black lesbian woman in the '90s, all while working on her film and chasing down "The Watermelon Woman." It begins with a name: Fae Richards

The film pulls off this impossible balance of being about Black women's representation in cinema while delivering an effortless hangout comedy. The cast is primarily queer simply because those are the people in Cheryl's circle. The film includes actual history, such as Black-owned clubs in Philly from the '20s and '30s, such as The Standard.

The film blurs the line between reality and fiction in a few ways. The street interviews seem authentic, as one person takes a dig at Rosie Perez and the film includes a brief "Sorry Rosie" interstitial. Cheryl Dunye interviews her real-life mother about "The Watermelon Woman." It also features appearances from several notable queer personalities, including most controversially, Camille Paglia, who argues in favor of the "mammy" figure.

In the other direction, the film injects segments from the fictional Plantation Memories. The filmmakers invented this movie solely because of budget constraints, as they couldn't afford access to archival footage. But if you've seen films from the era, you know that the invention is a thin veil to real life. We also see "artifacts" from Fae Richards' career — stills and photographs that give the subject a greater sense of authenticity.

Overall, the film was a little disheveled around the edges, but its authenticity and breezy flow made up for it.
