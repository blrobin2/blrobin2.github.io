<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qaTwm/detail">Asian Cinema Challenge 2023 | 44/52 | Directed by Hirokazu Kore-eda</a></b>

<b>月曜日 (Monday)</b>

Two men, Kawashima and Mochizuki, ascend the stairs of a rundown building.  Kawashima talks about a client who has only talked about sex for the past three days. Ultimately, that client chose a memory of a vacation with his wife. They meet up with the rest of their team to celebrate the 18 clients they sent through last week. This week, however, they have 22 — 8 for Kawashima, 7 for Sugie, and 7 for Mochizuki.

The bell rings. From the æther, clients enter and check in, awaiting their turn in the lobby — they appear as shadows, identifiable only by their names. While waiting, they swap stories and small talk. Then, in groups of three, they are sent to their respective conference rooms. Mochizuki and Shiori invite their first client, Kimiko Tatara, and officially let her know that she died yesterday.

Each client gets a private room for the week, during which they must select their most meaningful or impactful memory. The staff will then recreate the memory on film to screen on Saturday. After the screening, the client will move on, taking only one memory with them. Some clients are reticent to relive their past, recalling only painful memories. Others are awash in moments.

The film follows the staff as they work with their clients to find that one memory.

<blockquote><i>I'll only remember how I felt back then? I'll be able to forget everything else? Really, you can forget? Well, then, that really is heaven.</i></blockquote>

Though the concept is simple, the film integrates drama by also focusing on the counsellor's private lives and, at times, their time when they were alive. On the surface, the film has a fantastical-sounding story, but Kore-eda's fixation on human experiences on the fringes is central to the experience. The filmmaking follows his usual understated, often unscored style — the world-building is handled at the beginning, leaving the rest of the film dedicated to the clients' and counselors' stories.

The film began with Kore-eda interviewing over 500 people to ask about their personal memories. He also interviewed the cast, asking them to write down the memory they would take with them to the afterlife. The film combines footage of these interviews with scripted material and improvisation, creating a fantasy from documentary-style footage.

Kore-eda's grandfather had a neurodegenerative disease that caused the loss of memories. His grandfather eventually forgot faces, including his own. In Kore-eda's exploration of memory, he also wanted to touch on how, when we retrieve memories, we inadvertently manipulate them. A memory recalled enough times may change in small or more significant ways from the original version. Here, these changes occur between the client telling their memory and the staff recreating it.

Like a photograph, memory is inherently selective. The things we recall, our minds chose from the myriad more things that it chose to forget. Some memories live repressed, safeguarded by a mind looking to protect us. Some of us redefine a memory to overcome the trauma that the original occurrence caused us. And some are so polished that there is no way they represent any facts. We write our histories day by day, choosing and omitting through the complex processes of our minds.

This film is so special and moving. It fully embraces the subjective nature of documentary, creating these beautiful snapshots of people reflecting on their life, its bitters and its sweets. It doesn't burden itself with explaining its world — it gives us a frame through which we can experience people's fragility and vulnerability.

I don't know what memory I would choose. It wouldn't be from childhood. Perhaps it would be a moment when I felt truly loved. Regardless, I would choose something, if only for the possibility of forgetting everything else.

<blockquote><i>I searched desperately inside myself for any memory of happiness. Now, [...] I've learned I was part of someone else's happiness. What a wonderful discovery.</i></blockquote>

#### Stray Thoughts/Spoilers

- This movie, in particular, is one that came up, well, three years ago, when someone suggested I watch it. I've watched it now, so that person can finally rest their weary head.
- I love Hisako Hara as Nishimura, the older lady who collects things from nature.
- I feel like this movie influenced <i>Be Kind, Rewind</i>. The reason it ultimately fails is that it tries to recreate nostalgia rather than memory. This film captures the impetus to make movies — that movie captures the drive to make reboots and sequels.
