<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Channel 2021 | 49/52 | Josh and Benny Safdie's Closet Picks</a></b>

The Pollocks come over to Mavis' sister Barbara's home. Colin, one of her sons, is unbearably shy, while the other son, Mark, is an edgy dork. Mavis' husband, Frank, and Barbara's husband, John, talk about nothing while Barbara complains about Colin bringing in dirt.

Barbara and John live in middle-class suburbia, while the Pollocks are struggling in the East End, with only Mavis able to find work. Barbara and John's marriage is on the rocks—John can't stand Barbara's family, and Barbara can't get John to notice her or her unhappiness. Mark hangs out with skinhead Coxy, breaking shit and raging against the world.

The film follows these families as they navigate the hardships of 80s England.

<blockquote><i>— You like money, don't you?<br>
— Yeah, well, we all like money, don't we?<br>
— You believe in money?<br>
— Well, money's not something you believe in or not. It's a fact of life.</i></blockquote>

At the core of the film is power, and its total absence. Frank, Mark, and Colin do their due diligence — they go to the unemployment line, they look for work. But it doesn't matter. It doesn't change anything. Thatcher has royally fucked Britain's economy, doing away with any social obligation to its people, leaving them scrambling and angry.

Between Mark and Coxy, Mike Leigh sure loves his assholes. Mark, however, reveals a protective side toward Colin when it comes to principles — what are they? I'm not sure. But Mark is sure he's the only one with them. Coxy is a violent, racist shit. These plot lines arise from Leigh's unconventional filmmaking approach, building characters alongside the actors, doing rehearsals, and looking for a narrative in the middle of it.

Except it's hard to pinpoint what the narrative is here. We watch the Pollocks without a clear plot or drive. This is the meantime of their life. Andrew Dickson's gorgeous tack piano score gives each scene a haunting, disconnected feel. It, too, waits for the plot to kick in, or for something to happen.

So it's hard to evaluate. It captures a period and the angry young men it created. Everyone is giving top-notch performances. I think it has some captivating moments. I blame the Britishisms for me not connecting with it more. I guess I can't look for the redemptive in Mike Leigh's films.
