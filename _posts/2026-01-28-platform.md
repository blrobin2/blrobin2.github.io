<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qaTwm/detail">Asian Cinema Challenge 2023 | 39/52 | Chinese Cinema: Sixth Generation and beyond</a></b>

Feedback. Tuning instruments. People gather for a performance from the Peasant Cultural Brigade of A Train Is Going to Sháoshān, a state-approved story about a journey to President Mao's birthplace. After the show, the performers reconvene on the bus, waiting for Cui Mingliang, who they believe doesn't share their collective spirit. Mingliang creates a scene, annoying the group.

Zhang Jun waits for his mother to widen the bottoms of his pants to make bell-bottoms. She asks him to be useful, and Jun complains that he isn't allowed to share the workload with his brother. Jun believes The Party will take care of him and wishes he could move away to Guǎngzhōu, where his aunt lives. His father comes home, and he, Mingliang, and Yao Eryong help him load beams on the truck. His dad wonders if a laborer can work in bell-bottoms, but Mingliang sees himself as an artist, not a worker.

Yin Ruijuan and Zhong Ping wait for Mingliang and Jun to arrive as they go to the movies together. The girls make fun of Mingliang and Jun's bell-bottoms. During the movie, an announcement calls for Yin to come to the entrance. Her military officer father waits for her, telling her that seeing only foreign films is a bad influence. Mingliang sneaks out to check on Ruijuan, but slips away when he sees her father, who thinks Mingliang is a hooligan.

Later, Mingliang and Ruijuan meet up, wondering whether Jun's family knows he's dating Ping. Mingliang complains about how strict Ruijuan's dad is. Her aunt has helped arrange a marriage for her, which has incited Mingliang's jealousy. Later, Jun, Mingliang, and Eryong listen to Taiwanese radio and wonder what lies north of them.

The film follows Mingliang, Ruijuan, Jun, and Ping during Boluan Fanzheng (the reply to Mao Zedong's Cultural Revolution), Chinese economic reform, and the Age of New Enlightenment in China.

The film depicts youthful rebellion not as a rejection of Chinese reform but as an embrace of it. It is the ideological divide between their "Mao Zedong Thought" parents and the "Socialism with Chinese Characteristics" youth. It reflects on how modernization isn't inherently progressive, especially with how many people it leaves in its wake. Characters like Jun, Mingliang, and Ping embrace the cultural fads — Jun and Mingliang with their bell-bottoms, and Ping with her perm and makeup. Ruijuan, meanwhile, is more conservative, sticking to what she knows.

The film uses a series of static long takes as the primary composition model. The filmmaking feels rough around the edges, as if the static shots are a necessity rather than a choice. The shots are often taken from a distance, engulfing the subjects in their surroundings. This focus on setting highlights how the world around our characters changes, and it is those changes that force them to change along with it. It also sets us, the audience, up as outsiders who can only observe from our limited perspective. In this way, the film achieves a sort of vérité, or at least the illusion of it.

As the troupe privatizes, forcing the actors to invest rather than receive state funding, they travel more and see how Western influence changes everyone's lives. It's fascinating seeing this perspective on 80s China. I feel like everything I've seen has been from a Hong Kong or Taiwan perspective. I also haven't seen much (or anything, probably) of the Sixth Generation, which tends to focus more on urban life than the Fifth Generation, which tended towards rural life. We see what good comes from the changes — a family able to eat out at a restaurant for the first time — as well as the bad — a brother who, without the state's support, must sign away his life to continue working the mine for a pittance.

One recurring motif of the movie is the train — the symbol of progress. In the opening play, A Train Is Going to Sháoshān, the troupe forms a pretend train which symbolizes the onboarding of Maoist ideals. The troupe leader makes fun of Mingliang's train noises, but Mingliang has never seen a train in real life. One of the standout moments of the movie is when the characters see a train for the first time — the joy and excitement it brings, only to leave them in its aftermath.

<blockquote><i>An interminable train platform, an endless wait, an interminable convoy carries away my endless love. An interminable train platform, a lonely wait, my love is always about to leave. No love awaits me upon arrival. My heart lives on expectations, on eternal expectations.</i></blockquote>

I feel like the film's distancing effect makes it difficult for me to connect with the characters and their experiences. It shouldn't matter, but part of my scoring reflects my emotional response to this movie. Perhaps I can return to this one after seeing more from Jia Zhangke. Still, I recognize what makes this movie special, even if it doesn't hit me in the feels.
