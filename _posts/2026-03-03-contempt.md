<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 3/52 | Directed by Jean-Luc Godard</a></b>

<blockquote><i>"Cinema," said Andre Bazin, "substitutes our gaze for a world more in harmony with our desires."</i> Contempt <i>is a story of that world.</i></blockquote>

A voice reads the credits to us as we watch a camera dolly left, following a woman writing in a book. When the camera arrives, the cameraman turns it and points it down at us, the audience. Cut to Paul and Camille Javal in bed, discussing the upcoming Fritz Lang project Paul has accepted, an adaptation of the Odyssey. Camille considers going to her mother's while Paul is away, but Paul offers to let her visit the set if she wishes.

Paul meets with Francesca, the secretary to American producer Jerry Prokosch. They search the set for Jerry, who exits with a loud lament about the death of the film industry. The Odyssey is a mess, so Jerry wants Paul to write some new scenes, to give them film more — more sex, more… well, Jerry doesn't know. Paul doesn't think Lang will accept this new direction, but Jerry believes otherwise.

They meet up with Lang in the screening room, who shows them some shots from the film, mostly old Greek statues with primary color accents on certain facial features. Jerry perks up when there is a scene of a nude woman swimming in the sea. But after the screening, Jerry throws a fit, dismayed with the state of the film. Lang claims he has only filmed what is in the script, so if they want to change it, someone needs to rewrite it.

So, Paul takes the job. The film follows Paul and Camille as the film production strains their relationship.

<blockquote><i>— Why does money matter so much in what we do, in what we are, in what we become? Even in our relationships with those we love.<br>
— You may be right, but when it comes to making a movie, dreams aren't enough.</i></blockquote>

An odd film in Godard's filmography, it does not pursue the inventive explosiveness of his early New Wave works, instead settling into a classical, three-act structure. As a result, the film gives us more nuanced and developed characters than Godard can usually convey, particularly with Paul.

Godard did not want Bridgette Bardot in the movie, and certainly did not want to make a titillating rendition of Alberto Moravia's novel. Still, his financiers and producers demanded some, so he filmed the horny prelude, where Camille asks Paul if he likes different parts of her body. Though Godard denies it, it's hard not to compare the horned-up producer's behavior with that of this film's producers.

The film includes several discussions about poetry, criticism, and philosophy. Godard has Lang state Godard's own opinions on filmmaking, a sort of meta-folding as Lang has heavily influenced French New Wave directors, especially Godard. Characters also heap praises on Lang's work. It's all a little tiring, but it's thematic at points.

How you feel about the film will largely depend on your relationship to Godard. If you like his New Wave films, you might find this slow meditation too far a deviation. Otherwise, this might be the one Godard film you enjoy. As for me, I'm afraid that Godard stripped of his style doesn't offer much that excites me. More importantly, the treatment of Camille is abhorrent — I might feel differently if it wasn't so clear that Godard is on Paul's side.
