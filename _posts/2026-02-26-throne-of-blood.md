<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 2/52 | Directed by Akira Kurosawa</a></b>

<blockquote><i>Behold, within this place<br>
Now desolated, stood<br>
Once a mighty fortress<br>
Lived a proud warrior<br>
Murdered by ambition<br>
His spirit walking still<br>
Vain pride, then as now, will<br>
Lead ambition to the kill.</i></blockquote>

As the fog dissipates, a fortress appears: the Forest Castle. Before a council led by Lord Tsuzuki, a messenger proclaims that the North Castle has revolted and that a group of rebels, led by Lord Inui, has set several forts ablaze. Another messenger brings news that Captains Miki and Washizu have broken the siege. Lord Inui eventually surrenders. He sends his advisor, Odagura, to North Castle to kill the traitor and send for Miki and Washizu.

Miki and Washizu make their way through the labyrinthine forest under an unprecedented downpour. They stop at a hut they've never seen before. There, a spirit offers them a prophecy: Washizu will become lord of the North Castle, then someday, the Lord of Forest Castle. Miki will command Fort One, and his son will later become Lord of Forest Castle. In a flash, the hut disappears, replaced with piles of bones and armor.

Through the dense fog, Miki and Washizu eventually find their way to the castle. Lord Tsuzuki does as the spirit predicted and gives North Castle to Washizu and Miki Ford One. All appears well for the men and those who serve under them. Washizu has told his wife, Lady Asaji Washizu, about the spirit. Asaji encourages the ambitions that Washizu dare not entertain: that of becoming Lord of Forest Castle.

<blockquote><i>You must make a choice: stay here and await your own destruction, or kill His Lordship and make Forest Castle yours.</i></blockquote>

With the seeds of doubt and fear of double-crossing planted, Washizu considers his role in the fate of Forest Castle.

Like other Kurosawa jidaigeki, the film enlists a huge cast of extras and works on a massive scale, combining facets of Noh theater with his Shakespeare adaptation. Here, he transplants a decidedly English story into a Japanese historical context where it makes sense, trading the specifics of Macbeth for those that are more culturally appropriate. It's hard not to compare this movie to Kurosawa's color jidaigeki, <i><a href="https://boxd.it/cOptQL">Kagemusha</a></i> and <i><a href="https://boxd.it/6rytKB">Ran</a></i>, but this film set the blueprint for those films' scale.

The special effects are minimal but immensely effective. The most noteworthy is a scene near the end involving real arrows. In most cases, the filmmakers simply use what is already there as the foundation. They built the castle exteriors on Mount Fuji because the volcanic landscape and foggy atmosphere matched the intended mood. Then, for the courtyard shots, they brought soil from Mount Fuji to match the scenes. Oh, and the tree scene gave me chills!

What this movie has over those two is its length. At a little under two hours, the film makes excellent use of its time, moving briskly when the story needs it and dwelling when building atmosphere. The film also makes excellent use of absences. The fog wipes out much of the landscape, making it feel as though characters outside the castle are awash on a canvas. One of my favorite set pieces is when the soldiers meditate on the blood-stained corner where the traitor was brutally murdered. Even in monochrome, the blood screams from the walls.

Otherwise, we get so much of what Kurosawa does well: the set pieces are impeccably staged, the compositions are immaculate, and the art design is rigorous. You may not connect to any of the characters (a flaw present in other Kurosawa films as well). Still, the humanity on display and the brutality of ambition are all too familiar.

<blockquote><i>If you choose ambition, lord, then choose it honestly, with cruelty.</i></blockquote>
