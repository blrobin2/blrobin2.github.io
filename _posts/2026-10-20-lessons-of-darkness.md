<blockquote><i>The collapse of the stellar universe will occur like creation — in grandiose splendor.</i> — Blaise Pascal — Werner Herzog</blockquote>

Over misty, apocalyptic images of Kuwait after the first Gulf War, Herzog's narration recontextualizes it as if we were visiting an alien planet. Men in protective gear walk to a gas line where a fire blossoms, signaling to someone unknown to turn it off.

The film assumes you were alive in 1991 and constantly bombarded with news about the Kuwaiti oil fires. In 1990, Iraq invaded Kuwait over its alleged drilling in the Rumalia oil field. Kuwait was producing far above the set oil quotas, which led to a sharp worldwide decline in oil prices. After this, Iraq set fire to over 700 oil wells, storage tanks, refineries, and facilities in Kuwait.

Because the US kills leaders in West Asia, we can only speculate on Saddam Hussein's motives in setting the oil fields on fire. One theory is that it was punishment for overproduction, destroying the wells that caused it. Another belief is that the resulting smoke could aid Iraq in covert missions. Regardless, the cost was incurred.

Some environmental experts predicted a nuclear winter that would affect half the Northern Hemisphere. Others predicted the smoke would rise high enough to be rained out within a few days. In reality, the latter was closer, but the significant drop in air quality caused many respiratory problems in Kuwait and neighboring countries.

Over time, the oil spills have affected the land even more. The unburned oil created around 300 oil lakes that contaminated 40 million tons of sand and dirt. The resulting mix of oil, sand, and soot formed layers of tarcrete that covered around 5% of Kuwait's land. Vegetation slowly recovered, but some lakes have partially solidified. The oil continues to sink into the sand, increasing the risk of groundwater pollution.

The film itself covers none of this, but it includes interviews with war victims or surviving family members. Like Herzog's documentary <i><a href="https://boxd.it/6h9gO1" rel="nofollow">Fata Morgana</a></i>, the film seeks to alienate audiences by using images gathered on Earth that, to Herzog, do not feel like the Earth we know.

This "aesthetic" concern upset some audiences, as it feels irresponsible to the documentary ethos. But Herzog knows as well as anyone how the camera and documentary lie. Even observing creates bias, because the lens hides much more than it shows. The message is simple: we have made the Earth look like this. This is humanity's fault.
