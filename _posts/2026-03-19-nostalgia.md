<blockquote><i>I'm tired of seeing these sickeningly beautiful sights.<br>
I want nothing more just for myself.<br>
That's enough.</i></blockquote>

A car drives through the Tuscan countryside, where fog bleeds like watercolors on the landscape.  The car stops so that Italian translator Eugenia can stretch her legs. Russian poet Andrei Gorchakov reluctantly follows her as she walks a thin footpath into the fog and through a field to a church. There, women gather in supplication before a fresco, "Madonna del Parto" by Piero della Francesca, either wishing for a baby or to be spared one. The church's sacristan suggests she kneel, lest nothing happen for the others, but Eugenia remains standing.

<blockquote><i>— Why do you think it's only the women who pray so much?<br>
— I'm a simple man, but I believe a woman is meant to have children, to raise them with patience and self-sacrifice.<br>
— That's all she's meant for?<br>
— I don't know...</i></blockquote>

A parade of women carries candles and religious icons into the church, setting them before the fresco. They kneel before an icon of Mother Mary and pray to her to become a mother. One woman opens the blouse of the Mary icon, and a flock of birds emerges from the cavity within. Somewhere, perhaps in his mind, Andrei finds a feather twirling over a puddle, a shawl, and a champagne glass. He observes a woman with angel wings walking into his home back in Russia.

Back at the hotel, Eugenia berates Andrei, who was the one who wanted to see the fresco in the first place, but when it came to the moment, he didn't go in. While they argue over the untranslatability of art, Andrei has glimpses of his wife wearing a shawl and drinking from a champagne glass. Eugenia asks about Andrei's research into Russian composer Pavel Sosnovsky, who also traveled to Italy before returning to Russia. Once Sosnovsky returned, he took up drinking and eventually committed suicide.

<blockquote><i>Is this the end of the world?</i></blockquote>

The film follows Andrei's alienation and his reflections on the impossibility of returning to a past that no longer exists.

After <a href="https://boxd.it/drZQ0L" rel="nofollow"><i>Solaris</i></a>, Tarkovsky stopped working with cinematographer Vadim Yusov. While Yusov never got a clear answer for why, he extrapolated from Tarkovsky's avoidance that he was in political asylum from the USSR and believed Yusov had KGB agents around him who would force Tarkovsky back to the country. Cinematographer Giuseppe Lanci approximates Vadim Yusov's painterly style while maintaining a distinct perspective.

Here, Tarkovsky explores one's ties to one's home country, his own homesickness, and living life through translation. This film is the first that Tarkovsky made outside the Soviet Union. In one scene, Eugenia reads translated poems of Arseni Tarkovsky. Andrei tells her to throw them away, as poetry is untranslatable, as is the whole of art. Though we have Pushkin and Tolstoy, we will never fully understand Russia. Though we have Dante, Petrarch, and Machiavelli, we will never fully understand Italy.

<blockquote><i>For I knew that it had been no dream, but my reality. Yet I would die if I never returned to Russia, if I never saw again my homeland, the birches, the air of my childhood.</i></blockquote>

In Tarkovsky's later films, he explored the Western decadence that surrounded him in his exile. He wished to find that middle place between the individual's artistic expression and the transcendent power of beauty to elevate us out of selfhood toward something more. In this film, he suggests that it is our country's borders that prevent us from fully understanding one another. That is, by creating a place separate from home, we only create the conditions for nostalgia. We create distinct identities that further alienate us from our truest artistic expressions — the very thing that might save us.

<blockquote><i>What ancestor speaks in me? I can't live simultaneously in my head and in my body. That's why I can't be just one person. I can feel in myself countless things at once. […] We must fill the eyes and ears of all of us with things that are the beginning of a great dream. […] We must fuel that wish and stretch the corners of the soul like an endless sheet. If you want the world to go forward, we must hold hands.</i></blockquote>

For both this film and his final film, <i>The Sacrifice</i>, Tarkovsky wanted Anatoly Solonitsyn to play the lead. By 1982, however, Solonitsyn had died from cancer — the same type of cancer that would take Tarkovsky and his wife, Larisa, which they all likely contracted through exposure to irradiated chemicals while making <a href="https://boxd.it/2iK5pb" rel="nofollow"><i>Stalker</i></a>.

This film, like all Tarkovsky films, is one I'll have to revisit to get a better handle on it. I am not someone living outside my home country, and I feel like the full depth of this film belongs to those who live there. Still, the film is full of beauty and honesty — it's hard to hate something this nakedly personal.
