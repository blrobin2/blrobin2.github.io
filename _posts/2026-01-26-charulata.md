<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qaTwm/detail" rel="nofollow">Asian Cinema Challenge 2023 | 38/52 | Indian Parallel Cinema</a></b>

<b>Calcutta 1879</b>

Charulata embroiders the letter B surrounded by palm leaves on a handkerchief. B for her husband, Bhupati. The bell rings. She calls for the servant, Braja, to answer the door. Her birds caw. She peeks through the window shades and sees a man standing outside, shaking a can with his two dogs. She runs to her dresser to grab her binoculars. When that man leaves, she turns to another sound and watches people pass by.

As the sounds pass and the game diminishes, Charulata returns to the quiet of the house. She considers playing the piano, sauntering over to it. She opens the lid and plays a few keys, but stops when she hears her husband walking down the hall. She steps out to him, but he has already passed. He passes her again on the way back and doesn't acknowledge her existence. She brings up the binoculars to watch him walk away — just another passerby in her life.

That night, they sit together to eat. Bhupati brings up Charu's brother, Umapada, wondering if he had written. As Umapada has failed as a lawyer, Bhupati wants to bring him on as a finance manager for his political newspaper. She doesn't think Umapada is up for it, but Bhupati believes it'll be fine. It's only when she gives him the handkerchief that he recognizes how much time she has to herself, and how lonely she is. So, he decides to invite Umapada's wife, Manda, as well to keep Charu company.

Umapada does not share Charu's intellectual endeavors, so they end up playing luck-based card games. Bhupati's cousin Amal comes to visit, who has just finished college, and he intends to relax. But Bhupati has plans for him at the paper. When Amal and Charu speak, they find they have much in common. The film follows Charulata's relationship with Amal and with Bhupati.

The film explores the juxtaposition of art and politics, where Charu is wholly devoted to the arts and Bhupati to politics. Through writing, Charu experiences her life and her village as she'd only seen in her head. In this way, the portrait of her town is political. But Bhupati only sees the political through the lens of English politics. So long as the two don't see eye to eye, their relationship is distant, and they miss one another.

Her husband, well-meaning as he is, is too driven to prove the stereotype of "the idle rich" wrong. He doesn't want anyone to think he is lazy. When he thinks of Charu and her needs, he can only think in terms of money. But how could she be in want with such a wealthy and well-serviced home? His paper boldly criticizes the government, which is principally English-led with no Indian representation. After all, what does he have to lose?

This film was the first in Satyajit Ray's career in which he did not have a deadline for pre-production or the shoot. As a result, Ray spent months researching the film's historical setting and worked with Bansi Chadragupta to build sets that accurately portrayed India in the 1880s.

That opening sequence is so incredible. The way sound directs the action, the binocular sight gag, followed by that perfectly slow one-shot as she goes to the piano, only to stop after a second when she hears her husband. The camera work is so smooth in the way it controls the ebb and flow of the scene's energy. The scene on the swing — the moment where she sees the images for her story in her mind's eye! The movie is full of wonderful moments.

This movie enraptured me from start to finish. Madhabi Mukherjee has such a presence on screen, and the film has such a poetic pace. This movie is Ray at his technical peak, and I adore it.
