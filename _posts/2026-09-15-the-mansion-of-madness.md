When Gaston LeBlanc was a child in France, his parents died: his mother, they believe, was murdered by his father, who later died while under the care of a sanatorium. His tutor took him to North America, where he grew up to become a journalist. Longing to revisit his home country, he convinces an editor to send him there for a story about Dr. Malliard, who runs a remote mountainside asylum and has a distinctive way of treating the mentally ill he calls the "soothing system."

LeBlanc invites his friend and former classmate, Julien Couvier, and Julien's cousin, Blanche, to join him. Couvier is a good friend of Dr. Malliad and will handle the introductions, though he will not step inside the asylum because he fears the "mentally deranged." The three ride a horse-drawn carriage to the asylum. There, they meet armed guards, who hold them at gunpoint and refuse them entry. A captain tells the guards it's okay.

Once inside the gates, they see an Italian monk telling off a man dressed in red, carrying a pitchfork-shaped stick, and chanting the devil's name. Blanche falls ill due to nerves, and Couvier gives LeBlanc a card to show Dr. Maillard. A man dressed in unusual robes guides LeBlanc through the dense fog and into the asylum. Along the balcony run nude people with symbols drawn on their skin.

Scene after scene of peculiar scenarios greet LeBlanc before he finally meets Dr. Meillard, who gives him a tour and introduces his method of treating patients: he lets them indulge their whims and fancies. The film follows our characters as they endure the violent, cult-like ways the asylum operates.

Director Juan López Moctezuma found his initial footing in Mexico's avant-garde theatre movement, where he met Alejandro Jodorowsky. Moctezuma helped produce <i>El Topo</i>, which gave him the experience and confidence to direct his debut.

This film makes the weird choice of taking an Edgar Allan Poe satire about 19th-century medical practices. By creating a literal representation of the metaphorical imagery throughout, the film takes on surreal and uneasy vibes. Costumes and sets designed by surrealist artist Leonora Carrington further enhance the movie's uncanny feeling.

Despite how compelling this may sound on paper, the film's execution leaves something to be desired. Namely, the film is very loosely structured. Its surreal and absurd images carry us some of the way, but as the plot shuffles onward, the novelty wears off. Also, the film is cavalier with SA in a way that feels edgy for its own sake. Yes, it's scary to witness, but it's also triggering for little to no added depth.

Still, I enjoyed aspects of this, though they would work better with a stronger script.

#### Stray Thoughts / Spoilers

- The opening credits have a bizarre red-and-teal halftone filter applied over a scene of a woman on horseback brought through what looks like an above-ground cemetery. It turns out to be the setup to the movie's finale.
- The music also plays with us, giving cartoonish cues over scenes of horrific physical and sexual violence.
- The film's mix of horror elements and surreal imagery has influenced later filmmakers, such as Roman Polanski, who cited it as an influence on <i><a href="https://boxd.it/62nWTl">The Tenant</a></i>.
