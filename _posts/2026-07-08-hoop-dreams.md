<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 22/52 | Documentary</a></b>

In the Cabrini Green Housing Project, 14-year-old William Gates watches the Bulls play on television. His dream is to one day play for the NBA. William's mother and brother cheer him on, recognizing the joy basketball brings him and the skill with which he plays. In the West Garfield Park Neighborhood, 14-year-old Arthur Agee and his dad watch the Pistons play on television. Arthur, too, like so many kids his age, dreams of playing in the NBA. His father doesn't doubt that his son will make it.

Earl Smith is an insurance salesman by day, and a talent recruiter for local high schools as a side gig. He takes Arthur and his family on a tour of St. Joseph High School, where Isiah Thomas attended before attending Indiana University and then leaving early to join the Pistons. William, too, gets the opportunity to attend St. Joseph's. And it is he that St. Joseph's coach Gene Pingatore sees as having the same drive and charisma that he saw in Isiah Thomas.

William's older brother, Curtis, didn't make it, so he puts all his dreams into William, teaching and encouraging him. Their mother, more than anything, wants William to earn a high school diploma so he has options if the NBA dream doesn't pan out. William hopes that, if nothing else, St. Joseph's will get him into a college that works for him.

The film follows the two through high school as they grow, face hardships, and work towards their dreams of being in the NBA.

The filmmakers wanted to explore, as they called it, "the culture of basketball in the black community." They began by contacting Gene Pingatore, who put them in touch with Earl Smith. They all spotted Arthur and saw promise. It was Pingatore who brought up William, and the scope expanded. What began as a 30-minute short for PBS evolved into a near 3-hour documentary, cut down from 250 hours of footage.

The result is one of the richest and most human stories I've seen in a documentary.

<blockquote><i>Do you know what the system is saying to me? […] They don't care.</i></blockquote>

I don't know if it began with this documentary, but I feel like the idea that poor Black kids saw the NBA as their way out of systemic poverty was something that seeped into my subconscious at a young age. Growing up in the 90s, the NBA was a huge dream for any kids who were serious about basketball. For kids in the projects, their options were limited — with so little invested in their neighborhoods, so many kids join gangs, and playgrounds where kids play become premier locations to buy and sell drugs.

What wasn't as clear was how much the Black kids would have to perform. William and Arthur had to be toted off 90 minutes from their homes to a predominantly white school just to get the opportunity. The school only offered them partial scholarships. William was lucky to have a Cabrini-Green program help sponsor him. Arthur was less lucky. By falling in line, Williams is called a "thoroughbred of a player," but ends up with numerous injuries because they keep pushing him.

And that's where the movie is sharpest — when it looks at the machine that chews these kids up, all in the name of money. Spike Lee shows up to remind them that the basketball pipeline does not care about them. The only reason the NBA pays its players is that they make it money. If they don't win games, they lose their worth. When we catch up with Earl several years later, he sees what has happened to the kids he recruited for St. Joseph. He thought he was doing them a good turn, but the evidence points otherwise.

<blockquote><i>We don't even understand what we're doing to these kids. A kid not even 12 years old and you're baiting him to come to your school […] to play basketball.</i></blockquote>

But what makes this film special is the intimate portrait it draws of William and Arthur, and of the people surrounding them. We see how their support doesn't always help the way they think it does. We see their dreams rooted in William and Arthur's success — the win they never got to have themselves. Also, I loved watching William and Arthur cheer for one another. This film is not a tragedy — it's a slice of life, its failures and its successes. We see how hope propagates and redoubles when all seems lost.

I'm not a sports fan, but I watched and played basketball growing up. I can't say I fully understood what was happening in the games, but the storytelling pulls me along by showing me how everyone else is feeling. I only say that because this film takes something I care little about and, by empathetically focusing on these people, makes it one of the best documentaries I've seen.
