<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 26/52 | Directed by Michelangelo Antonioni</a></b>

Riccardo sits by his books. Vittoria stands by the window. They talked all of last night. Vittoria has already decided. Riccardo plays dumb. She sets their coffee cups in the kitchen. When they met, they were happy. She isn't happy anymore. She walks out, into the streets. Riccardo runs up in his car and joins her on foot, back to her apartment, to say goodbye.

Later that day, Vittoria takes a taxi to the Rome Stock Exchange, where she meets with her mother. Vittoria's mother speaks to Piero, her stockbroker. Piero makes a bid when the stock's price is low, then sells it when it rises for a profit. Vittoria tries to talk to her mother about Riccardo, but she can only talk about her profits.

The film follows Vittoria as she tries to figure out how to be happy, all while dodging Piero's advances and Riccardo's prying.

<blockquote><i>What can I say? There are times when holding a needle and thread, or a book, or a man — it's all the same.</i></blockquote>

The film explores the various ways we try to make ourselves happy: money, art, weight loss, nationalism, pets, travel, superstition, sex, booze. Everything here today could be gone tomorrow. Vittoria experiences others' identities, seeing how she fits in — this includes a blackface performance as Vittoria does a racist interpretation of an African tribal dance. That part is super jarring, and thankfully only lasts for a minute or two.

Antonioni's directing style is not for everyone. Long silences. Composed frames. Despair on top of despair. His films feel like intellectual wrestling with the limitations of thought when it comes to the human heart. With all our advances, humanity has yet to figure out what makes us happy.

But what helps Antonioni is his lack of irony. He's not making stylistically challenging or overtly clever films. He's still fixated on notions of beauty and meaning, even if he arrived at a similar sense of alienation and ennui that pervaded the era. I feel like his sense of place grounds the audience and makes us feel like we are there, or that it is swallowing us up.

It's important to note that the relationship supposedly at the center of this movie doesn't start until halfway in, and isn't much until the last few minutes. Piero has fully bought into capitalism, and you can see all the ways it has stripped him of his humanity. When a drunk steals his car and drowns, all he can think about are the dents and what it will cost him. He sees Vittoria as an investment, and tries to move quickly. But she doesn't know what she wants, and she won't let Piero choose for her.

<blockquote><i>I wish I didn't love you, or that I loved you more.</i></blockquote>

The score is dissonant, present only in the opening credits, briefly at an hour and forty-five minutes in, and in the final moments, where the joys and promises of modern love meet the fragile hell of modernity. It's those final few minutes that really make this something special.
