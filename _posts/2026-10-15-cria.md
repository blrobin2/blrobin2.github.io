One night, eight-year-old Ana hears her father, Anselmo, in bed with another woman. Ana hears Anselmo's gasping. The woman rushes out. Ana enters the room to find her father dead. She spots a glass of milk and brings it to the kitchen to wash it out. Ana's mother, María, chides her for being up so late, but Ana runs into her mother's loving embrace before racing to bed.

Irene, Ana's older sister, brushes her teeth. Rosa, the housemaid, brushes Ana's younger sister Maite's hair, then Ana's. María takes over brushing Ana's hair, talking to her without moving her mouth. Ana comes to, and Rosa is still brushing her hair. As she leaves, Ana recounts the night's details to Irene, including María sending her to bed. Irene reminds her that their mother is dead.

Downstairs, men in Spanish military regalia line the coffin. Ana refuses to kiss her father as her aunt requested, instead running to her grandmother's side. A couple enters, Amelia and Nicolás — Ana recognizes Amelia as the woman who ran out of her father's bedroom.  Ana goes into the basement and runs to a tin containing white powder. As an adult, Ana begins to explain what the white powder means.

Twenty years older than the young Ana we see, she reflects on why she wanted to kill her father, and cannot come to a conclusion that makes sense. The film follows Ana as she jumps through time, reflecting on the promising life her mother gave up to become a mother, all while enduring the increasingly painful life under her Aunt Paulina's stern care.

<blockquote><i>The only thing I remember very clearly is that at the time I was convinced my father was responsible for all the sadness that embittered the last years of my mother's life. I was convinced that it was he and he alone who brought about her illness and death.</i></blockquote>

Since we experience the movie through Ana's perspective, we find ourselves bound to her imagination. Often, she sees images of her father and mother—are they memories or imaginings? During the wake, Ana rolls her grandmother's wheelchair outside. On an apartment roof, she sees herself standing on the ledge, then jumping.

The film came out at an unusual time—Franco lay dying, and director Carlos Saura had a sufficiently high profile that Franco's censors feared what would happen if they cut his movies. As a result, despite its surreal and psychological underpinnings, the film can be blunter in its allegory.

It also helps that actress Geraldine Chaplin, who plays both Ana's mother and Ana as an adult, contributed financially and creatively to the film. The story centers on how women are raised and socialized—the roles forced upon them, what games are okay, and what is "too masculine." We also see the cost María pays to be a mother and wife, especially to a man as singularly cold and manipulative as Anselmo.

<blockquote><i>There's nothing. Nothing. They lied to me. I'm afraid.</i></blockquote>

The director uses childhood misery to reflect the misery of living in Franco's Spain. As children, we have no sense of direction other than what the adults in our lives force on us. They fill us with their fears and neuroses, projecting their experiences as facts of life rather than their own traumas. We only understand death as an absence, and sex as a taboo we must learn about behind closed doors.

<blockquote><i>You're still too young to realize how serious certain things are.</i></blockquote>

The movie features almost no music except for the song "Porque te vas" by Jeanette, which Ana listens to repeatedly. The contrast between the upbeat sound and the depressing lyrics underscores Ana's naivety. The title refers to a Spanish proverb, which translates to "Raise ravens, and they will pluck out your eyes." One way of interpreting this is that raising children with cruelty will come back to avenge you. Applied to a nation, it suggests something similar.

<blockquote><i>I still can't understand people who say that childhood is the happiest time of one's life. It certainly wasn't for me. Maybe that's why I don't believe in a childlike paradise or that children are innocent or good by nature.</i></blockquote>

I need to watch this one again as soon as possible.
