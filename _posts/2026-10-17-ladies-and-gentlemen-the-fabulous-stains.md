A news report features teenager Corinne Burns being fired for calling out her hometown of Charlestown, Pennsylvania. Thousands of teens write to the station in support of Corinne, prompting a follow-up interview. The interviewer points out Corinne's dire situation. Corinne calls herself Third Degree Burns, and she's the lead singer and manager of The Stains.

She attends a concert with her bandmates, where a British punk band called the Looters opens. The main act is a faded glam band, Metal Corpses, whom Billy chastises for being washed-up. Lou, the Metal Corpses singer, demands that tour manager, Lawnboy, find a new opener for the tour.

After the show, Corinne approaches Billy, hoping to audition The Stains. Lawnboy overhears and offers The Stains a country-wide tour. Little does he know that The Stains have only ever practiced three times. The band members aren't exactly thrilled, but Corinne sees it as their way out.

The film follows the tour, the hostilities, and what success means.

<blockquote><i>You could be different from all the other girls. Suckers! Be yourself! These guys laugh at you. They've got such big plans for the world, but they don't include us. So what does that make you? Just another girl lining up to die.</i></blockquote>

The film began when screenwriter Nancy Dowd approached music journalist Caroline Coon about the burgeoning punk movement in the mid-1970s. Caroline saw feminism as punk's next big movement and built a script around it. Paramount brought on record producer Lou Adler, who had produced artists like The Mamas & the Papas and Carole King, to direct.

Adler and Dowd's sensibilities clashed immediately, resulting in a tonally dissonant film, especially in the ending. At one moment, we get wonderfully oddball women who give the movie a sense of humor grounded in authentic teenage angst. But the camera betrays the movie's messaging, ogling at the statement and muting it for male audiences.

This clash led Dowd and Coon to leave, and the film sat shelved and unfinished for a few years. Adler tacked on an ending that totally redefines what success meant for Corinne and The Stains. But in some ways, the meta reinforces Dowd's intentions. What is the line between adoration and exploitation? And who gets to define that?

<blockquote><i>— It doesn't make sense to wear a see-through blouse and no bra and say, "I don't put out."<br>
— That's not what it means. It means don't get screwed. Don't be a jerk. Don't get had.</i></blockquote>

Recognizable actors include real-life members of The Sex Pistols, The Clash, and The Tubes. There is also an incredibly brief appearance by a young Brent Spiner as Corinne's fry-cook boss. Laura Dern sued her mother for legal emancipation so that she could be in the movie. The film acted as a precursor to the riot grrl scene and influenced bands like Bikini Kill, Courtney Love, and Kate Nash.

When the movie shines, it is powerful. When it doesn't, blame the men who coopted the movement to line their pockets — it's a tune familiar to many women-driven bands.
