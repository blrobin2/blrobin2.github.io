Karl Åge walks through his neighborhood, deep in thought and uninterested in what goes on around him. Arriving back home, he helps his wife, Regitze, set up tables and plates for a garden party. Karl worries that Børge and Ilsa won't come. He flashes back to an earlier evening when Regitze calls Børge a bastard for how he talks about his wealth and employees.

Back in the present, Børge and Ilsa arrive at the Åge home. Børge and Karl have a beer on the porch. Smash cut to a swing club during WWII, where a young Børge introduces Ilsa to his old friend Karl. While the others dance, Karl drinks a beer alone at the bar. Another night, Karl sees Regitze and falls for her immediately. Overcoming his shyness, he approaches her and asks her to dance.

Between dances, Regitze introduces Karl to her best friends, Rikard and Annie. Børge and Ilsa leave Karl to spend time with Regitze. Karl and Regitze stay past closing and dance together. Returning to the present, another carload of friends arrives, including Rikard and Annie. Another car has Karl and Ilsa's son, John, and his family. We briefly flash back to an 11-year-old John jumping on his dad to wake him up.

The film moves through time as Karl recounts the life he has built with Regitze and the changes they have experienced as they grow old together.

<blockquote><i>Tonight, it's like all the doors are open. And I keep wandering in and out of them all.</i></blockquote>

The film uses elegant transitions, where a character's movement in one scene carries into the next, often taking us to a different point in the past. The moments are sudden and non-linear, like memory jumping between moments in time. By introducing this format choice within the first few minutes, the audience quickly adjusts to the sudden shifts.

Karl is reserved and timid, while Regitze is much more outgoing and impulsive. She is ready to provide her opinion, while Karl is willing to suppress it to keep the peace. They feel each other's differences, especially as they grow older, with Regitze wanting to travel and Karl hating the idea. In those changes and differences, Karl worries he is holding Regitze back.

But looking back, we also see how much he belives in Regitze. Her heart leads her to fight for others. Even when Karl is reluctant himself, he firmly backs up her sense of justice. She invites the wealth of an open heart into Karl's life, and into the lives of all she touches. The original title of the movie is "Dancing Regitze," because it is her unwavering energy that sustains the beauty of Karl's life.

Actor Frits Helmuth and his son, Mikael, portray present-day and young Karl. The casting is brilliant, because my mouth dropped when I saw young Karl. Ghita Nørby and Rikke Bendsen's Regitze and young Regitze do a tremendous job of matching each other's little mannerisms. I love the way Frits Helmuth and Ghita Nørby look at each other.

The score prominently features a jaw harp. It feels like a weird detail until near the end of the film. That's all I'll say.

The film has a tenderness to it. On paper, it has a similar format to other late 80s and early 90s Oscar bait, using a grand history to focus on small lives. If I had seen this when I downloaded it, in my early 20s, I might have hated it and called it saccharine. But time has softened me, and while I love a challenging movie, I have a lot more room in my heart for sweetness and sincerity.

I laughed. I cried. My heart is warm. I'm also sleep-deprived, which tends to make me more sensitive to sentimentality. Regardless, I'm looking forward to revisiting this one.
