I'll let the movie tell you its deal:

<blockquote><i>For 400 years, the ancient art of astrology has slumbered, ignored by scientists, feared by the church, the tool of [Romani], charlatans, and thieves, waiting for the astrologer. Alexei Abarnel is the astrologer, a brilliant, hard-headed scientist who used the enormous leaps in astronomy and the most advanced computers to refine the art of astrology into an exact science.</i>

<i>The astrologer wanted to use these new tools for the benefit of mankind. He formed an organization called InterZod, an organization which could monitor the astrological potential, or the zodiacal potential, as Alexei called it, of everyone in the world. InterZod changed the course of history. Alexei tried to change destiny itself. He dared to investigate the ultimate biblical prophecy: that the virgin birth would reoccur and the child shall come again.</i>

<i>There was, however, one problem that not even Alexei had foreseen. There were two forces trying to father that child. One, of course, was ultimate good. The other was equally ancient, but undeniably evil.</i>

<i>It is now 10 days before the second coming. But whose child will it be?</blockquote></i>

For the next 20 minutes of the movie, we travel to Arlington, NY City, India, Bethesda, and London. The headquarters discovers the zodiacal potential for evil of an Indian man, named Kajerste. Kate has her fortune told by a tarot reader. Kate misstates her birthday, and the tarot reader is convinced that a strong presence has a hold over her. Alexei asks Congressman Joe Harwell to support his upcoming endeavors.

<blockquote><i>God help the man who would stand in the way of someone truly destined.</i></blockquote>

Not to be confused with <a href="https://boxd.it/1PcwDb">the once-lost 1976 <i>The Astrologer</i></a>, this film occupies a similar cult space, though it is ostensibly more horror. So little actually happens in the movie that it's hard to call it much of anything.

Alexei seems like the bad guy straight away — he's manipulating his wife, Kate, who seems very smitten by him, but the feelings don't appear reciprocal. We see Kajerste in proximity to wanton murder, mutilation, and mayhem, but we also see InterZod making fake videos of Kajerste. The movie even suggests that there is some misdirection at play. But…

This movie has so much dialogue. I don't mind a talky movie, but so much of it is recapping what we've just seen or restating facts the film gave us mere minutes ago. Filmmaker James Glickenhaus didn't know any editing techniques and didn't know how to convey ideas except through dialogue, so the movie, by the director's admission, is mostly dialogue.

That said, I eventually found it strangely compelling. It had set up some threads and left enough dangling for me to follow along. Of course, then it gets to the end, and it feels like it's either trying to make for an ambiguous ending or set up for a sequel. I can't decide whether I like how it ends.

The film is on the Video Nasties list, and I can see why early on. The use of actual footage of brutally murdered children is heinous. And they show it more than once! And for minutes at a time! Also, Monica Tidwell, who plays Kate, ends up nude at random points for no reason in particular.

I don't know if John Cameron, author of the book on which this is based and director James Glickenhaus' father-in-law, is also James Cameron's brother of the same name. It would make sense only because Brad Fiedel, who did his first score for this movie, later went on to score several James Cameron movies. The score is easily the best part of the movie — it genuinely rules.
