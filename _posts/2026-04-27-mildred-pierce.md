Swelling, melodramatic music plays over the credits, with crashing waves acting as transitions between title cards. The music calms as we settle in an LA beach house. Then, gunfire. Inside, bullets fly as Monte Beragon collapses and dies. His final words are calling out for his wife, Mildred. A car speeds away from the house. Mildred walks the rainy docks at dusk — her eyes teary, and her furs iconic. She grasps the handrail to step over when a police officer stops her.

As Mildred walks away, Wally spots her from the window of his cocktail lounge and invites her in for a free drink. She downs a shot of Wally's cheap house liquor and invites him back to the beach house for the real deal. Wally worries about her husband, but she says he isn't here. Meanwhile, Monte's body lay in a dark room, unnoticed. Mildred steps away to change, but sneaks out, leaving Wally to think she's in the bedroom.

Wally soon realizes that he is locked in the house. When he accidentally knocks over a lamp and finds Monte's body. The phone rings. Wally unplugs the phone. He throws a chair through a window as police drive by, and they stop him in his tracks. A taxi drops Mildred off at her home, only for her to find detectives waiting for her. They take her back to the station. Later, Mildred's first husband, Bert Pierce, comes in.

Eventually, the detectives bring Mildred in to apologize and to let her go, saying they have found the killer: Bert. Mildred is certain that Bert didn't do it, but the murder weapon was Bert's, and he confessed. Mildred tells the story of the past few years and her children, whom she would do anything for.

The film makes several changes to the novel on which it is based, ironically adding a murder and punishment to comply with the censorship code of the time. The novel is less a thriller than a psychological melodrama — the film heightens several elements to turn it into a thriller, while removing some of the historical context that makes the financial circumstances of the story more plausible.

It feels like the film is chasing a sort of <i>Stella Dallas</i> "look at this suffering woman" thing. Director Michael Curtiz initially wanted Barbara Stanwyck for the role of Mildred Pierce, but she passed on it. The film also tries for an unusual juxtaposition of summery LA story and a film noir sensibility. It doesn't always fit together, but the contrasts help the film feel like a descent into darkness.

I've made some complaints, in large part because I'm rating a classic lower than average and want to explain myself. But there is still plenty to appreciate about this movie. Most notable is the focus on Mildred and her plight, bringing the film noir sensibility into the trappings of motherhood. She is a woman who would do anything for her daughters, including taking on any menial work she can. She is one of the untold women who struggle in silence to make ends meet, far from the cozy lifestyle of the rich who occupy her hometown and friendships. Of course, Mildred can fulfill the American dream and buy her way into success. But that success comes at a cost.

Joan Crawford is dynamite in this movie, bringing a veneer of style and grace that doesn't necessarily fit the idea of Mildred, but certainly fits the story. In her efforts to make her daughter, Vera, feel special, she spends money and takes care of her appearance, lest Vera be embarrassed by her mother. In this way, the film seems to call into question that silent generation mentality of building wealth for their children so that their children don't have to work as hard as they did. The resulting entitlement is the Boomers, who are currently on track to bring humanity as close to hell as they can for one more chance at appearing wealthy or worthwhile.

The men in Mildred's life are absolutely dreadful. Bert is a lazy, self-defeating cheat. Wally is a disgusting, self-serving asshole who will throw everyone under the bus to make a buck. And Monte… well, I'll leave that for the audience to discover. These men are all too prideful to do what Mildred does: sacrifice some dignity for the sake of caring for her family. While this characterization of men was popular in "women's pictures" of the time, these men also reflect the other side of the American dream. These temporarily embarrassed, soon-to-be millionaires will posture and exploit before they make any honest move on someone else's behalf.
