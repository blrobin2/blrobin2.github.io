<blockquote><i>It's like everything had changed in the blink of an eye. One minute, we were quiet, and the next, we all danced.</i></blockquote>

Lale's teacher hugs her one last time, promising to write from Istanbul. Lale has tears in her eyes. Lale's sisters wait for her. Selma gets in the van for a ride home, but Sonay suggests walking home on such a beautiful day. They walk with other classmates along the Black Sea beach, when the boys start splashing them. So they all jump into the water and play games like chicken, still in their school uniforms.

When they arrive home, their grandmother chastises and beats them one by one, starting with Sonay. Ms. Patek told their grandmother that the sisters were  "rubbing their crotches and masturbating on boys' necks." The sisters fight back, confused and angry at their punishment. Nur runs outside and sets a chair on fire — it touches their backsides, should it be punished too?

Their uncle Erol comes home. When he hears, he is furious with their grandmother, his mother, for being so lenient and letting them be children. He tells the sisters they have been bitches, pulling their hair. He takes them to a doctor to have their virginities checked. If any of them had lost it, then the family would not have been able to marry them off.

So, their grandmother forbade them from leaving the house. If they go out, they go with their grandmother, dressed in conservative clothing. No makeup. No computer. No phone. They will not attend school. They will learn to cook, clean, and sew. The film follows the sisters as they figure out what to do.

<blockquote><i>Nobody believes me when I say I'm a virgin. I want to disappear.</i></blockquote>

The film explores the amorphous time between girlhood and womanhood. In many ways, men are the ones who define that line based on what they want — age of consent, age of marriage, and so on. When a woman no longer aligns her life with men's expectations, we might define adulthood as when the brain is fully formed, which is closer to 25 or even 30.

The film invokes immediate anger on behalf of the sisters. We feel their rage, their rebellion, and we root for them to find a way through it all, even as their choices become self-destructive. The TV shows are all about women worrying over their value to men. Lale loves football and wishes to attend a match, but her uncle forbade it because of the men present. The film is not entirely miserable, though — we see moments of joy and beauty, however fleeting.

The film has strong <i>Virgin Suicide</i> vibes, but with conservative Turkish repression instead of the conservative American kind. Still, the film does plenty to differentiate itself and deliver a compelling story.
