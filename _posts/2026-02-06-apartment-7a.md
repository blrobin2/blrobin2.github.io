Terry is a dancer in New York City. During a Broadway performance, she injures her ankle. The stage turns into an operating room as surgeons put her under. Her injury leaves her with a painful limp that she treats with pills she buys from a street corner trumpeter. She comes home to find her roommates arguing over whether she should stay now that she can't pay rent.

Terry finds a casting call for Alan Marchand's The Pale Crook. At the audition, her reputation as "the girl who fell" precedes her. The director forces her to do the move that caused her to injure her ankle over and over again. Marchand then asks her to act like a pig, but Terry refuses to humiliate herself. She downs her last few pain pills, and her roommate approaches her.

<blockquote><i>— Being on stage isn't worth this.<br>
— Of course it is.</i></blockquote>

Terry follows Marchand, hoping for another chance to impress him. She watches him enter the Bramford building and follows soon after. She asks the doorman to see him, but she seems a little loopy. She hears strange noises that the doorman doesn't hear. Terry stumbles outside, bumping into Minnie and Roman Catevet before throwing up on the street corner. They take Terry up to their apartment, 7A, and put her to bed. Soon, the two allow her to live rent-free in their other apartment, 7 G.

From there, the film more or less follows the plot beats of <i>Rosemary's Baby</i>.

<blockquote><i>It's not our falls that define us. It's what we do afterwards.</i></blockquote>

The film is, obviously, a prequel to <i>Rosemary's Baby</i>. If you'll remember from that movie, Terry is the recovering drug addict that Minnie and Roman took in. She wore a tannis root pendant. One night, she jumps to her death. After Minnie and Roman schmooze with Rosemary and Guy, Minnie gives Rosemary the tannis room pendant.

In this film, we get the 20 minutes of backstory from above, as well as the hint that Roman and Minnie have tried this number out on multiple women before. Otherwise, I think it's pretty clear what is going to happen to Terry. But, in case it isn't clear to you, I'll discuss it below in the SPOILER section. That's also where most of the praise for the movie will be. ¯\_(ツ)\_/¯

Where the movie frustrates is in how content it is to replay, at times line-for-line, moments from the previous film. I understand the prequel's desire to press the nostalgia button, but this much isn't necessary. Compare with another prequel from this year, <i>The First Omen</i>, which deftly hits the nostalgia button at the edges and in its final moments while leaving plenty of room for interpretation.

This movie is at its most compelling during Terry's post-drug nightmare. Rosemary dreamed of what was happening: she was raped by the devil. Here, it's a classic Broadway-style musical number AND then the devil rape. It also interprets the devil in a fun way.

Also, no one really gives a good performance beyond Julia Garner and Marli Siu. Dianne Wiest and Kevin McNally are forced to impersonate characters we already know. Weirdly, Jim Sturgess acts like he's indebted to someone else's performance, but he's just being weird and out of touch. At least they didn't digitally overlay the previous actor's faces over them.

Overall, I don't love this movie. While I appreciate how it updates its approach to reproductive politics, it doesn't do enough to differentiate itself from <i>Rosemary's Baby</i>.

#### SPOILERS

It's in following what happens to Terry that the film earns some merit. Because what we get is a contemporary take on the reproductive rights the original was considering pre-Roe v. Wade, and this movie is considering post-Dobbs. In both films, the lead gets mysteriously pregnant. In <i>Rosemary's Baby</i>, however, she would never consider getting rid of the baby. While here, Terry never wants it, knowing that, whatever the case, having a baby would definitely ruin her dance career. But this is still about the 1960s.

It's also a compelling idea to essentially combine Rosemary and Guy. Guy is complicit in the whole thing because it furthers his career. Here, Terry finds her career assisted. Beyond getting the role, she also gets "medical" help for her leg and other little anti-miracles. In this way, the film can play with the "slept her way into the role" trope, but with the caveat that she doesn't remember it. Though we know it is a devil child, all she thinks is that her producer raped her, and now everyone expects her to have the child.
