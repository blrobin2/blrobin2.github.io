Apu leaves university early with a letter of recommendation. His teacher encourages him, if nothing else, to continue his writing. Back home, his landlord comes to collect three months of rent, giving him until the evening to gather the necessary rupees. Apu finds a book on his shelf to pawn to pay the rent.

Apu looks for work, but finds himself overqualified and the jobs underpaying. If nothing else, a short story of his will be published in a literary magazine. That night, while practicing his flute, he hears a knock on the door. His school friend, Pulu, complains that no one knew he had left school.

Pulu promises to get Apu a clerical job after Apu comes with him to Khulna for the wedding of Pulu's cousin Aparna. Initially, Apu rejects the idea, celebrating his freedom and lack of responsibility. But when Pulu laments Apu's inexperience with love, Apu accepts the call.

The film follows Apu as he learns what love is.

Like all of the Apu trilogy, this film captures elegant slices of life, painting an intimate portrait of India. Before pawning a book, Apu removes a leaf he uses as a bookmark — a plant from his childhood home. By going to Khulna, we return to the rural life that dominated the first film. This moment hints at later returns and cycles of renewal.

We also get small glimpses of how the British occupation infects their lives—protests, strikes, and language laced with English phrases. At the wedding, the wedding band plays a rendition of "For He's a Jolly Good Fellow" on Indian instruments.

Throughout the film, Apu wrestles with Western values of freedom and independence that conflict with the responsibilities he accrues. He is working on a book based on his own life, which sees him falling in love, but he does not understand the sacrifice that love demands. We watch him experience it — the sweetness, the heartache, and what remains.

The film makes a choice that forces us to reconsider our relationship to Apu up to this point. We have watched him endure hardship after hardship, only to experience the one that finally breaks him. As a broken man, he makes choices that few in the audience would agree with, and we must wait and see if we will ever be on his side again.

The sound design is so perfect. The cinematography is the best that it's been for the series, with compositions that stir the heart. The performances are all tremendous. Satyajit Ray needed to step away from Apu's story to learn the filmmaking lessons needed to land it. And he did — this trilogy is a masterpiece.
