The film opens with an Italian newsreel detailing Italy's invasion of Albania on April 7th, 1939, which made Albania an autonomous part of Fascist Italy's Empire. According to the newsreel, the Albanian people gladly welcomed Italy's armies, as Italy built rural hospitals and schools, provided housing for peasants, and established "civilization."

<b>Port of Durrës, 1991</b>

Hundreds of Albanians protest at the port, demanding permission to leave for Italy. An Albanian man named Selimi crosses the gates and greets Italians, Fiore and Gino. As they leave, Albanians pour through the gate despite military efforts to hold them back. They drive to meet a friend of Selimi's, who will sign a contract to become chairman of their new shoe company.

Gino and Fiore meet with the contract signer at a hotel, with Selimi acting as translator. They sell her on the idea of Alba Calzature, a shoe company that uses local factories, workers, and supplies but is built with Italian craftsmanship. Gino looks at her passport and realizes that she is also named Selimi. Fiore sends the woman away and tells Selimi that they need someone with no connections, who will do as they say.

And so, they go out to look for someone old and poor. They meet former political prisoners living in disheveled quarters. Their go-between finds an older man named Spiro Tozai, who has no one. Tozai believes he is twenty years old. When Fiore and Gino arrive at their new factory, where none of the machinery works, they reveal what we suspected: they are running a front meant to take money from the state.

Fiore leaves for Rome, leaving Gino to care for Tozai and get everything sorted. The film follows Gino as he immediately loses track of Tozai. Gino must chase him down, all while learning who he truly is.

<blockquote><i>Are you Albanian or Italian? Tell me what the fuck you are!</i></blockquote>

The film isn't shy about portraying Italian contempt for Albanians. Fiore believes Albanians are children who will believe anything Italians say. He also believes that Communism softened the people, and that they think The State will take care of everything instead of pulling themselves up. Gino yells and pushes them around like they are too stupid to understand him. When Tozai pees himself in Gino's car, Gino forces Tozai's face into it like a dog.

Of course, the film makes it clear that we are not supposed to agree with them. They are scam artists, and Fiore and Gino's father ran a similar scam in Nigeria two years earlier, buying second-hand electronics and selling them out of their television factory. Italy gave them a grant. They never made any televisions.

The film also shows that most people wanted to escape Albania for Italy. Through the character of Tozai, the film emphasizes how "oppressed" Italians were post-WWII, which is why Tozai forged papers to go into hiding as an Albanian. Similarly, the film presents Italian culture as the de facto cultural influence, with Albanians watching Italian TV and listening to Italian music. One kid on the bus wants to immigrate to Italy to become a Christian.

For its primary characters (the Italians), the film does a nice job of characterization. It isn't afraid to show what so-called democracy does for its people, but it does so at the expense of humanizing the very people it uses as its foundation.

#### Probably Unnecessary History Lesson

Jumping from 1939 to 1991 is kind of insane, but the film assumes an Italian audience familiar with the history up to this point. So, let's break down some of that history. I gladly welcome any clarifications or corrections.

After a politically fraught independence and WWI, Albania lacked an established government and internationally recognized borders, leaving it vulnerable to neighbors such as Greece, Yugoslavia, and Italy. They attempted to establish a republic, but Fascist Italy created sustained pressure to establish a constitutional monarchy. In 1939, they invaded and subsumed Albania.

In 1943, the Italian Fascist regime collapsed, and Nazi Germany took over, but by 1944, the Red Army bolstered Albanian Partisans, forced Nazi Germany out, and Albania became a communist country. Almost immediately, the CIA attempted to stir anti-Communist sentiments, but ultimately failed to make any headway. In the early 60s, however, Albania severed ties with the USSR because of Khrushchev's revisionism.

China had made a similar split with the USSR, and the two supported each other until the early 1970s, with China's Three Worlds Theory, which Albania saw as a Chinese alliance with American imperialism. By 1977, Albania and China split ties. While Italy's Marxist-Leninist Party sided with Albania, the country at large remained firmly pro-capitalist.

With so many alliances dissolving, Albania found itself isolated from the world at large. Enver Hoxha, who ruled the People's Socialist Republic of Albania for four decades, died in 1985. Ramiz Alia, who succeeded Hoxha, followed the trend of many Eastern Bloc countries, softening on Communist ideas and opening up to economic reforms.

By 1990, revolts started in Shkodra. Alia worked to reform Albania's political system, opening it to multiple political parties and forming his own Democratic Party. Many Albanians saw the immediate social and economic instability that resulted. Fearing civil war, some migrated to Italy, seeing it as a symbol of the West.

By 1991, Italians saw the incoming Albanians as illegals. They set up detention camps in the south and deployed soldiers along the Puglia coast. Overall, Italian citizens held an overwhelmingly negative view of Albanian immigrants. News articles often conflated Albanians with organized crime or spread outright misinformation, such as blaming them for cholera outbreaks.
