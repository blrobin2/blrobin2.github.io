<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qaTwm/detail" rel="nofollow">Asian Cinema Challenge 2023 | 47/52 | Shaw Brothers Studio</a></b>

<blockquote><i>Without you, the world has become unbearable to me.</i></blockquote>

In the Tang dynasty, the former Emperor Xuanzong stands alone and forlorn in his chambers. Two palace attendants come to bring him to the West Palace, a ruling brought down by the new Emperor, Xuanzong's son. Xuanzong refuses, and the attendants leave. Once they leave, he gazes upon a statue of Kwei-fei, wondering where she is.

The film returns to when Xuanzong was younger, as he plays an original composition on his lute with chamber musicians. The musicians ask for a more uplifting tune — after all, isn't he the most fortunate man in the land? But Xuanzong has lost his wife, and he composed this somber tune in her memory. The ministers arrive to bring the Emperor to the Chancellor to discuss state matters. Xuanzong wants to finish playing the song, but they insist that he must honor his primary responsibilities as Emperor. The ministers try to stir Xuanzong's interest in women, even having a topless Bodhisattva statue made to get him all riled up. But for Xuanzong, no concubine can replace his wife.

Master An Lushan arrives, also disheartened that they cannot find a woman for Xuanzong. He overhears a woman singing in the kitchen, Yang Kuo-chung's youngest cousin. Though Yang tries to dismiss An Lushan's interest, he sees in her face more than a passing resemblance to Xuanzong's late wife. This woman is Yang Kwei-fei. And why would these men care about Xuanzong's love life? Yang Kuo-chung is tired of life as a restaurateur and wishes he could become a politician, take bribes, and live a more relaxed life. An Lushan also wishes to have political influence as a ministry leader or more.

Kwei-fei knows they are using her as a political tool — no different than when she worked the kitchen. Still, she follows through on the training they put her through so that she may gain favor with the Emperor. The film follows Tang Kwei-fei's journey from birth as consort to the end of her life.

<blockquote><i>Someday a peaceful spring will come, when the plum blossoms are in full, glorious bloom [...] I pray with all my heart for the arrival of such days.</i></blockquote>

So, technically, this film is not a Shaw Brothers film, in that they were called Shaw & Sons at the time. The film is a collaboration between them and Japan's Daiei Film, and is about Chinese history — hence why a HK company has a Japanese film in their repertoire. Not only that, they have one of two color films that Misozuchi directed (the other being Taira Clan Saga).

The film uses Chinese history to explore political corruption. While the men who use Tang Kwei-fei have their corrupt motives, even Xuanzong himself is an ineffective leader focused more on his personal pleasures than on political rule. Even when he knows a concubine is using him to gain political favor for their family, he fights against his own laws that prevent it. He paints the example that the other men wish to follow.

And what does Kwei-fei want? If we look at her through her words, then it would be to be loved by someone as kind-hearted as Xuanzong. But as the movie progresses and she sees the effect her family has had, she wants to step away from her position and protect the empire. But the law does not allow her to dictate the direction the empire takes.

The film uses beautiful sets, layering deep settings with lush matte painting backgrounds. Mizoguchi is smart in how he uses color, primarily assigning it to certain characters or roles to help us distinguish them and assign motives to their colors. His restrained, pastel palette isn't exciting, but the elaborate set dressing more than makes up for the difference.

I think what keeps me from scoring this higher is Kwei-fei's characterization, or lack thereof. Her motives are almost always altruistic, which I suppose reinforces how easy a pawn she is, but I felt like other characters had more nuance to their motives. Still, this film is a moving tragedy rich in symbolic and thematic elements that I found compelling.
