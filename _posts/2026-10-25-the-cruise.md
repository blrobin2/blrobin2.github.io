A ragtag group of passengers boards a small ship for a cruise down the Vistula River. Two men without tickets decide to board anyway. One distracts the control guard at the beachhead while the other distracts the guard, then reverses. For the second guard at the gangplank, the two pretend like they have important business with the captain.

We meet several passengers: the pervy Engineer Mamoń and his oblivious wife, Christine; the disgruntled Sidorowski; and several others through silly visual gags. I'm not sure what happens to one of the stowaways, but the other interviews with the captain, who mistakes the stowaway for a cultural coordinator from the Polish United Workers' Party.

The captain announces the stowaway's role and asks that everyone cooperate with him. With a crowd at his disposal, he awkwardly introduces himself and listens to the passengers' complaints and rants. Soon, they nominate him as Cruise Control, allowing him to dictate their activities on the cruise.

The film follows the stowaway as he uses the crew to create an elaborate song-and-exercise routine for the captain.

The film was made with a relatively small budget. They used a single monochrome camera with a shoddy lens. So, to accommodate zooms, they had to move the actors physically. Because the film has almost no professional actors, the camera operator tied a string to their legs to draw them closer to the camera. The crew coined this the "Turkish zoom." This also meant recording on-set audio, which created its own set of challenges.

The slight plotting gives the movie a very relaxed feel. The pacing is rather disjointed, as several scenes, though scripted, relied on non-professional actors. As a result, the filmmakers made some adjustments and improvisations. Still, the awkwardness has its own humorous, authentic quality, much like early episodes of The Office.

While the film uses several sight gags that don't require context to find amusing, its intelligence comes from its satirical underpinnings. The stowaway, as Cruise Control, begins creating a secret routine to perform for the captain. He creates committees that evaluate song and dance using the same rhetoric as the Polish People's Republic, applying dialectics and cultural critiques of banal acts.

Because the film had to pass through Cultural and Artistic committees, this openly anti-government film underwent edits and revisions. Still, they allowed limited distribution, lest anyone think the government was suppressing a brilliant artist. Interestingly, they demanded that a physical actor play the captain to avoid people thinking the unseen captain was a stand-in for political figures.

Still, despite the setbacks and revisions, the film became a cult classic, the first of its kind in Poland. The filmmakers themselves don't fully understand how that happened. I won't pretend to understand all the cultural nuances. But the film's farcical tone and distinctly Polish awkwardness manage to transcend cultural borders.
