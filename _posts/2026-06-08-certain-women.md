<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 19/52 | 2010s</a></b>

Bells jingle. A train whistle blows. The cold, painterly landscape of Livingston, Montana, allows the train to pass gently through it. The radio station warns folks to keep an eye on their dogs' water bowls, lest the water freeze and swell their dogs' tongues.

<b>Tome \*\*\*.5</b>

Laura Wells lies in her underwear as Ryan enters the room, getting dressed. After he leaves, Laura heads into her law office, where her client, Will Fuller, waits impatiently, despite having set up no meeting. Will has been trying to sue his workplace for injuries he sustained for over eight months, but because he accepted an initial small settlement, he forfeited his right to sue. Laura has set Will up with another lawyer for a second opinion, but she suspects that lawyer won't tell him anything different. When the other lawyer explains this to Will, he accepts it readily.

On her way back, Laura gets a call from Ryan, saying he's in a tough spot and may not be able to see her much. At the same time, Will's wife kicks him out of the car nearby, and he immediately jumps into Laura's car. On the way, Will says, "The only thing left to do is get a machine gun and kill everyone." Laura threatens to kick him out, and he shuts up, only to cry a little later.

Laura and Will have resigned to life, letting it roll over them like an uncontrollable wave. They believe they struggle, but nothing seems to change. When Will changes those dynamics, Laura feels compelled to act, despite thinking to herself, "This is insane, right?" With Laura, we don't know what initial choice led her here. But for Will, we do: the moment he took the settlement. Also, with Laura, we don't know what is holding her back or why she continues down the path she does.

<b>Native Sandstone \*\*\*.5</b>

Gina Lewis returns from an early morning run to the plot of land where she and her husband, Ryan, intend to build a home. Nearby is the tent they have set up to live in, along with their teenage daughter, Guthrie, while they build the home. Things seem stiff between Gina and everyone else — Guthrie and Ryan seem barely able to stand her, but Gina persists. They load up the car to spend Sunday at their current house, but Gina and Ryan decide to make one more stop on the way home.

They stop at Albert's home, talking on the way about sandstone collected by pioneers that was once part of a schoolhouse. Now, the sandstone lives in a pile on Albert's land. Gina looks over the pile before letting Albert know they're there. Gina intends to persuade Albert to see the sandstone to them, and Ryan plays along.

Gina has the bitter "grin and bear it" demeanor of married women who are miserable but refuse to change course. She feels like she's having to figure it out all by herself and doesn't understand why no one is helping her. Ryan knows his life would fall apart without her, but cannot face her, so cheats with Laura. They all seem to use one another under the pretense of family, hoping that if they charge through, they'll arrive where they want to be. Albert, however, shows them in his old age that there are always unfinished projects, especially when it comes to homeownership.

<b>Travis, B. \*\*\*\*.5</b>

Jaime is a ranch hand at Haddan Ranch in Belfry, tending to horses with her adorable corgi but is otherwise alone. As night falls, she makes dinner in the microwave and watches TV. Restless, she drives into town. She spots a few cars parking at a school. Curious, she follows and finds a seat in the classroom. A young lawyer named Beth Travis enters and starts a class on school law.

After the class, Jaime follows Beth out. Jaime offers to take her to a diner before she heads out. There, Beth complains about the 8-hour round trip from Livingston to Belfry, where she works a regular job. Beth asks if Jaime will keep coming back. Jaime offers to show Beth the ranch sometime.

Jaime is shy, but kind. Her loneliness feels unfair because it seems the only reason she's there is because she never found the voice to ask. In meeting Beth, she feels the urge to speak up but finds it difficult to get through to her about just how alone she is.

<b>Overall</b>

Though the film doesn't present itself as an anthology film, it essentially is. The three stories overlap in places, but they are told separately, each based on a separate Maile Meloy short story (which I have used as the section titles). As the stories don't originally connect in any way, Reichardt's script finds ambiguities in them and revises them to connect them all.

Like other Reichardt films, the story is content to linger in the ambiance of the space, breathing life into the surrounding world and allowing it to ebb and flow. We also have moments of silence between characters, where we can watch their minds work through just what choices in their lives brought them here.

The film focuses on the loneliness in the lives of different women. Each case is wholly different from the other, but each feels helpless and unaware of what they can change. Each of the actors portraying "certain women" brings a specificity that speaks to something universal in how we relate to each other.

Reichardt is also incredible at drawing comedy from truth, letting everything play grounded and believable so that when something even slightly absurd is called out, the film gives way to humor. Sometimes, the funniest thing about life is the story we tell ourselves, said out loud against the contrast of real life.
