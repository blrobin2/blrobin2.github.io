<b>Let's clear this up first — the title refers to a gun, as in it'll get the character in jail if the police catch him with it.</b>

At a police station, Inspector Johns and Lieutenant Lawrence meet with Marilyn Gregor, who posts bail for her brother, Don, whom they arrested for carrying an unlicensed handgun. Johns is convinced that Don is involved with a gangster named Vic Brady. They release Don, but they don't give him back the handgun.

Back home, Don grabs a hidden snubnose revolver from a hollowed-out book and bumps into his father, Boris, entering as he leaves. Boris seems fully aware of Don's association with Brady, but is more perturbed by the facial reconstruction surgery he had to do on a patient who was in a car accident. That is, until he discovers the snubnose missing.

Don meets with Brady at a bar called The Hunters' Inn, where Brady gives Don his cut from a robbery in which Brady left Don holding the bag. Brady spots Johns and Lawrence entering the space and has Don pass the gun to him. They leave for a job to rob the payroll at the Monterey Theatre — Don is having doubts, but Brady threatens Don if he backs out now.

At the theater, Don and Brady force the nightwatchman to open the safe at gunpoint. While counting the money, bookkeeper Miss Willis leaves and stumbles on the robbery. Don shoots and kills the nightwatchman while Brady shoots Miss Willis in the back. Police sirens send the two on the run, Don in shock over killing someone.

It seems the two crooks are doomed, what with the police having their pictures on file. That is, until Brady figures out how to make use of Don's father. Will it be enough to escape?

The film is the first acting role for Steve Reeves and one of the few to feature his actual speaking voice, as he made most of his movies in Italy and therefore was dubbed. The film is also Herbert Rawlinson's last, as he died of lung cancer the night after shooting wrapped. Clancy Malone, who plays Don, only ever made this movie because he was a grocery delivery boy who just wanted to try acting. It was also the only role for Theodora Thurman, who later pivoted to a much more successful radio career.

The score is a near-continuous flamenco guitar and piano, creating an anxious white noise that suggests film noir but doesn't match the banality of the acts it accompanies. It's nondescript enough to avoid being too obnoxious, but if you listen for it, it gets pretty tedious.

The version of the film I watched doesn't include the insertion of Cotton Watts performing in blackface. The decision wasn't Wood's in the first place, and was likely a cynical appeal to the Deep South, where the filmmakers released the movie.

Some folks consider this film Ed Wood's best, if only because it lacks the unintentional humor of some of his other films. We still get plenty of incongruities and tedium, but Ed Wood is hardly the only director to have committed these moviemaking crimes on such a small budget.
