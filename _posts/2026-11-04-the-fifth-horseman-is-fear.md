Dr. Braun walks through the Registration of Confiscated Property where he works. Shelves of plates, liquids, books, sewing machines, instruments — all confiscated from Jewish people. A room of men, one going through folders, calling out names and serial numbers. A wall of confiscated clocks—stolen time. Braun answers the phone. Someone calls about a Dutch painting to confiscate and its location. nd so it goes until the end of the workday.

Dr. Braun walks home in silent urgency. Patriotic music plays as he runs into a man. Braun turns away, but the man seems to follow him. A sustained eye, and the man disappears. Back home, screaming and whistles from the nearby soccer field fill the otherwise sparse flat. A neighbor, Mr. Fanta, freaks out when he learns Mrs. Kratochvilova is keeping rabbits as a food source. He worries what will happen if the authorities discover this.

Outside, a young boy named Honza watches a man struggle to ride his bike and tumble over. Honza laughs at the man until he stumbles into the apartment. Honza asks him what is wrong. Outside, a car calls 44-8-11. The man stumbles up the stairs to a neighbor, Mr. Sidlak. Sidlak comes to Dr. Braun, asking for help with the man's gun injury, claiming it was a hunting accident. But Braun is not a surgeon, and he isn't allowed to practice medicine.

Still, Dr. Braun reluctantly helps. His hands tremble in fear. He'll need morphine, but he has none. He removes the bullet—isn't that enough?

<blockquote><i>They haven't shot me in the back, no. But it's much worse, for they've left me my brain, which has just been considering some notion of duty as to whether or not I should try and get morphine for God knows whom. Me. Me, precisely. But that's absurd, isn't it? Why me? Why?</i></blockquote>

The rhythmic pulse of the movie creates the feeling that we are moving forward, but it is uncertain where until the injured man arrives. Everything is hurried but meaningless, busy to avoid trouble. Everyone avoids thinking because someone has decided to think for them and decide everything for them. This is life under authoritarianism. Nobody wants this, but nobody knows how to overcome it. So hatred and apathy become the default.

Dr. Braun assumes the worst. He knows his age and his health. He has seen enough to decide. His only mission is to survive. Like many, he has resigned himself to fear, believing he will survive as long as he succumbs to it. But this injured political dissident challenges his sense of self. He tries to rationalize away inaction, but something deeper propels him forward.

The film manifests the fear through illusions and manipulations. Dr. Braun sees people watching him and hears whispers from nowhere in particular. The movie gives us both the illusion and reality, but slowly weans us off the reality until we are as submerged in the tension as Dr. Braun. And he isn't the only one. Everyone around him is in a similarly disordered state.

The movie does a tremendous job of keeping the viewer on their toes without disrupting the story or concepts. There's a scene in a nightclub where a woman starts moving about, everyone watching her as though she were about to break out into a musical number—the music keeps trying to kick it up, but instead, men in white coats grab her and put her in an asylum.

<blockquote><i>Silence. This is silence. I can't stand the silence anymore. I can't stand it. Can't stand it.</i></blockquote>

While this isn't the Czech New Wave at its most confrontational, its combination of surreal elements and historical context packs a powerful punch.
