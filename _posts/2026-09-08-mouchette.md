<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail" rel="nofollow">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 38/52 | Kelly Reichardt's Top 10</a></b>

Mouchette's mother cries. "What will happen to them without me?" She walks away.

The film opens with Arsène setting up a bird trap, and another man freeing the trapped bird and bringing the trap home. Arsène, angered by the man's actions, drinks at the local bar. The bartender, Louisa, sends Arsène away. Mouchette's father and older brother unload alcohol from their truck for the bar. The barkeep pays the man, Mouchette's father, pouring shots for them as well.

At home, Mouchette cares for her dying mother, preparing hot water so that her mother may soak a washcloth and keep it against her skin for warmth. Mouchette's infant brother cries out, and Mouchette tends to him as well. Her father and brother return home drunk. Her drunk father makes racecar noises as she tries to sleep.

Mouchette comes to school in a raggedy dress, hole-strewn stockings, and clogs much too big for her feet. The kids stand to sing, but Mouchette does not. The teacher shoves Mouchette to the piano, pushing her face into the keyboard, and plays the melody until Mouchette sings it. Crying, she goes back and tries to sing the melody, but can't hit certain notes. The kids laugh.

We continue to follow Mouchette, seeing all the ways that life is unfairly cruel to her.

The film explores how cruelty begins in the land and governance, and reverberates through the people. Hunters torture animals with painful traps. People drink because what else is there to do? Sickness takes people when they are needed most. Mouchette is not just a node for receiving that misery, but propels it forward in whatever small ways she can.

<blockquote><i>I hate them. I'll stand up to them all.</i></blockquote>

Bresson lets the camera do so much of the storytelling, with sparse dialogue passed between the characters. What can be shown will be shown, but with a poetic composition that favors the smallest detail. Despite being around 80 minutes, the film is often considered slow cinema—time seems to crawl, and moments linger, but it all feels intentional.

The original author, Georges Bernanos, wrote the book and Mouchette's miseries based on his personal experiences during the Spanish Civil War. Like <i>Au Hasard Balthazar</i>, the film contains that nihilistic streak that seems to revel in pessimism. That said, it feels more motivated here—then again, it's been long enough since I've seen <i>Au Hasard Balthazar</i> that I may be giving it undue negativity.

Scenes of hunting bookend the movie — ways of mirroring the way the innocent suffer for the sport of men.

One aspect that is tough to rectify is when Mouchette embraces an SA because it is the only sort of physical affection she's received — her father only hits her, and her mother is too weak to do more than touch Mouchette's cheek. Obviously, there's a huge "Not How Women Work" flag going off here.

Mouchette does experience small glimmers of light, such as a boy at the fair who flirts and is kind, or a woman who gives Mouchette some soup and bread. Almost all end as quickly as they come, with someone intervening to ensure that no love is experienced, even if that intervener is Mouchette herself.

With the opening framed in Mouchette's mother's tears, we may view the film not as a literal set of events, but as the fears that she has for her daughter, living in the world both of them know all too well. Fever dream or not, the impact is the same on the viewer.
