For a brief stint in my childhood, I was convinced that my life was a TV show, and that when I went to sleep, the credits rolled over my sleeping body. Sometimes, I would talk to myself as though addressing the camera. Other times, I would imagine a narrator describing the subtext. That belief went away, but the dissociation that motivated it stuck around, peaking around high school, when I would have auditory hallucinations.

My queer awakening began in kindergarten, and was so thoroughly repressed by my religious upbringing that I have spent most of my life feeling like I don't exist. Sure, I could play whatever character the scene called for — wasn't it Paul in his letter to the Corinthians who became "all things to all people?" Dissociation was my way of making myself real in all the ways life could not.

This film is not real, and it wants you to know that. The backdrops are hand-painted. The gore is cartoonish in the explosive CGI blood and absurd kills. The technology in the Camp Miasma movies is anachronistic. The movie warps and distorts like poorly-kept film. What is more real is Little Death, the killer at the heart of the story. Kris chases what Billy experienced in the first Camp Miasma movie — to be the one who experiences the little death, and the one who gives it.

The needle drops were custom-crafted for me, with Donna Lewis, Sade, and the most iconic use of The Counting Crows ever put to film. It is full of images that feel both nostalgic and wholly new. Like <i><a href="https://boxd.it/6ufQsf">I Saw the TV Glow</a></i>, it feels like a message crafted for me right now, at this point in my life. I am both the one who experiences this life, and the one who watches.
