<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 17/52 | 1990s</a></b>

<blockquote><i>There is no telling how he arrives in this dream, only that he feels his body swaying and uncertain, and rolls into it like fog chased away by clouds. He looks up and finds himself elsewhere. He looks hither and thither and sees not a path, but a boundless and infinite sea of flowers.</i> — Han Ziyun, <i>The Sing-Song Girls of Shanghai</i></blockquote>

<blockquote><i>The most elite pleasure quarters in late 19th-century Shanghai lay hidden in the city's British concession. These enclaves were known as "flower houses."</i></blockquote>

Around an oil lamp-lit table sit flower girls (sex workers) and some wealthy patrons, including Masters Wang, Luo, and Hong. They play drinking games and smoke opium. While most of the men are excited and energetic, Wang appears forlorn, present but not engaged. They talk about the flower girls they've fallen in love with.  Wang steps away from the table with Crimson. Once out of earshot, the other men talk of how he has been "cheating" on Crimson with Jasmin. Crimson found out and sent maids to beat Jasmin. Whispers pass among the flower girls.

Focus passes to Crimson, of the Huifang Enclave. When she first met Wang, she had many visitors. But once Wang became her patron, they stopped coming. Wang promised to take care of Crimson and her debts. Now, 2.5 years later, Crimson does not mind that Wang sees Jasmin, only that he is so secretive and has not paid the debts. Wang has brought two friends in for his defense, but it has only angered Crimson. Wang begrudgingly promises to pay the debts, but it only frustrates Crimson further.

The film branches out to the other flower girls and the relationships they have with wealthy patrons.

The movie does not tell you why you are here. We are told flower houses exist, and then we enter one in medias res. Through this experience, we must hold onto the words and images, like servants collecting gossip, and learn the confined and dependent nature of their lives. So much of the dialogue focuses on what happens outside the flower houses, with the wealthy men bringing in the stories, telling them to the flower girls, and by extension, us.

The lingering cinematography holds long shots, letting scenes play out organically. In the opening scene, the camera sways to capture the full room, but always settles back on Master Wang, living from his perspective. The tight movements keep the viewer constrained to the same boxes that the women are — this is their world, and it is ours for two hours. The long fades to black suggest that the time between scenes could be minutes or years.

We also see how fleeting their time as a flower girl is, hoping to be married off before they get too old to attract callers. The experience is reminiscent of <a href="https://boxd.it/egcJiz"><i>When a Woman Ascends the Stairs</i></a>, which has a similar focus on the hardships of being in work that revolves around wealthy, often married men. These girls are bought at age seven or eight and raised by the house mother to become courtesans — it is through this job that they pay their debt to the house mother for buying and raising them.

The film went to great lengths to recreate old Shanghai. The sets and costumes are beyond immaculate. The filmmakers opt for candles and oil lamps to light the spaces, giving them all an immense warmth. Nearly every shot has someone smoking opium, by this point a drug the British forced China to legalize through two wars during the century.

Ultimately, the film is a nightmare more than a dream — women's lives hanging in the balance of men's feelings and whims.
