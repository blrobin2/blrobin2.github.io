<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 20/52 | Directed by a woman</a></b>

We open on a vineyard in winter. A zoom that feels like it never stops centers on a farmer gathering branches fallen from winter trees. He stumbles across a dead woman in a ditch. The police come to photograph her contorted body, questioning the farmers and speculating on what happened. The best they can tell, she froze to death.

<blockquote><i>No one claimed the body, so it went from a ditch to a potter's field. She had died a natural death without leaving a trace. I wonder if those who knew her as a child still think about her. But people she had met recently remembered her. Those witnesses helped me tell about the last weeks of her last winter.</i></blockquote>

A flashback takes us to a winter on the beach and Mona Bergeron emerging from the icy water. We take on the perspective of some asshole teens who spy on her. One boy challenges the other to approach her, but he races off on his motorbike instead.

The sun sets, and Mona hitches a ride with a trucker. Through context, we can tell that she has been squatting in empty vacation homes. When the trucker realizes she isn't going to fuck him, he drops her off. We realize that he is telling this story to a fellow trucker at a stop.

Some men discover Mona sleeping in a hen house. We cut to her walking the street to a house, where she asks if she can fill up a water bottle. She asks another house for matches to light a cigarette she just rolled. The woman who filled Mona's water bottle talks about how freely Mona lives and how she would like to live.

And so the cadence goes as we catch glimpses of Mona's last winter through the eyes of those who crossed her path.

Sandrine Bonnaire is so talented as Mona. She carries a woe that she never shows to others, appearing cheery and carefree at times. She picks up what odd jobs she can, doing as little and taking as much as she can in the process. She's restless, but so tired of running. Everyone she meets has an idea of her, sometimes even an ideal. We only get to know Mona through these stories and interviews. Sometimes they are conversations—at other times they speak directly to the camera, as though in a documentary.

Varda put together Mona by actually giving rides to and interviewing women like her, assembling a portrait of a type of woman she was more regularly encountering in the world. In this way, she blends the real with the fiction, telling something authentic through a story structure built around indirection and unreliable narrators.

The film does an excellent job of not making Mona a saint. For some folks, she will be a schemer evading the working world. We don't know how she came into this position, only a story that she tells. If the story is true, she becomes even more reckless, and if not, she becomes even less trustworthy. In this way, the film invites its audience to form an opinion of Mona the same way that the folks she encounters do.

The way the film edits across segments is so fascinating. The segment ends with a fade-to-black, and we start wholly somewhere new, finding the context. We never know what happens in between. The moments within are almost like smash cuts, as though each disparate memory were lined up one after another. It keeps an organic sense of disorientation. The score is unsettling, unresolved, furious and lamenting but aimless. Even the camera in these long tracking shots seem to abandon Mona.

Overall, the film is a remarkable character study and audience study. Who deserves our charity? What line could they cross that would make you turn them away? The answer should be simple. And yet…
