<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qa26g/detail">Cult Movie Challenge 2019 | 3/52 | Anime</a></b>

<b>1982</b>

In a Tokyo office building, Taeko receives approval for a 10-day vacation to the countryside.

<b>1966</b>

All of Taeko's friends are going to the countryside for the summer. When Taeko returns home, her mother says they aren't going anywhere for the summer, especially not the countryside, as they don't have any family there. Taeko's older sisters, Nanako and Yaeko, talk about a place in Atami. At first, Taeko isn't too interested, but the talk of hot springs and a mermaid bath stokes her curiosity.

<b>1982</b>

Taeko calls Nanako to tell her that she's heading up to the farm in Yamagata to see her husband's family. She's going to help pick safflowers. Their mom is mad at Taeko for not going on a date she set up for Taeko. Nanako agrees that, at 27, Taeko should be thinking more about her future rather than taking random vacations.

<b>Both 1966 and 1982</b>

Nanako suggests that Taeko might meet someone cute on her vacation.

<b>1966</b>

Taeko takes the overnight trip with her grandma (not her sisters), and tries as many baths as she can, passing out in one from overextending herself. The trip over, she has a long, boring summer vacation awaiting her. She and her family try fresh pineapple for the first time, but it is visibly unripe and hard. The sisters give their pieces to Taeko, who wants so much to like them, but doesn't enjoy them.

At lunch time, Taeko and Suu agree to help each other out — she'll drink his milk if he eats her radishes and onions. They'll still be allowed to leave one thing on their plate. That is, until busy-body Tsuneko brings up the fact that people aren't clearing their plates at a school meeting, suggesting that everyone should have to clear their plates.

<b>1982</b>

As Taeko travels, memories from her childhood that she hadn't thought of in years flood her mind. For Taeko's sisters, 1966 was the year of the Beatles and art school — the high point of their youth. For Taeko, it felt like just another school year. And yet, her 5th-grade memories keep coming back to her, as though her younger self has something to tell her.

<blockquote><i>I didn't intend to bring my fifth-grade self along with me on this trip, but clearly she was along for the ride.</i></blockquote>

The film uses two distinct styles to distinguish between Taeko's childhood and adulthood. The childhood follows a more traditional anime style, where animation is done before voice acting. The adult version is more detailed and realistic, with animation based on voice acting, resulting in more subtle and accurate facial movements. Because the film jumps back and forth between these time periods with little to no indication, the animation style helps reorient the viewer.

The film explores expectations and disappointment, both as a child and an adult. It can be simple things, like an unripe pineapple, or more lasting effects, like a mother who could never be proud of her daughter's accomplishments. Or, the belief that nature has nothing to offer unless you have family there.

Later, the film also explores the isolation of city life, contrasted with the communal nature of country life. Her brother-in-law's cousin, Toshio, considers himself a peasant and seeks ways to feel solidarity with peasant culture worldwide, such as listening to Hungarian folk music. He loves his work and being involved in work with so many living things.

In this way, the film interrogates the alienation from Japan's 80s economic boom, as well as the global drive that pushed people out of nature and into cities. It is through Taeko's time in the country that she comes to understand what her childhood self missed — the connection with nature and the sustainable agricultural models that capitalism has all but erased.

<blockquote><i>— You know, there's no job that isn't tough, so you do what you have to do, and in the end, somehow you get by. Do you think it's the same in the city?<br>
— Yeah, but I find a lot fewer people there who really live for their work.<br>
— And how about you, Taeko?<br>
— I suppose I am one of them.</i></blockquote>

The film does a tremendous job of capturing the uncertainty and awkwardness of childhood, and the way memories can reignite the excitement and frustrations from our younger years. I find the idea of your childhood self speaking to you deeply moving, as I have spent these past few years trying to nurture that younger self who felt so stupid and unlovable. All my younger self really wanted was to feel like they belonged somewhere.

While the film is more grounded than most Studio Ghibli films, it finds moments that capture magic — the floating feeling of a first crush or the dream of becoming a famous actor. It may also be Ghibli's most overtly political film, at least that I've seen.

Please stay through the credits!
