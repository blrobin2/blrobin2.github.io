<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 47/52 | Based on a book</a></b>

<blockquote><i>In July of 1945, Japanese forces were on the ropes in Burma as well. Our unit fled from mountain to mountain on foot. We were desperate to cross the border and enter Thailand.</i></blockquote>

Over the Burmese countryside, the sounds of gunfire, screaming, jets, and bombs drown one another out in a sea of cacophony. Then, silence. A Japanese unit stumbles through the brush, exhausted. One soldier asks for a song. Captain Injouye, a music school graduate, calls for PFC Mizushima to play his Burmese-style harp as he leads the unit in singing.

At one stop, Mizushima changes into traditional Burmese attire, handing his fatigues and weapons to Private Kobayashi. He will perform forest recon, using his harp to signal any danger. While on patrol, some thieves corner him. The unit hears the "all clear" song. They find Mizushima wearing only banana leaves, the rest of his clothes taken.

That night, they take shelter in a local village. Things seem peaceful as they sing and chat, but they discover British troops nearby. Captain Injouye encourages the men to sing "Home! Sweet Home!" and act like nothing is wrong. While singing, they slip out to secure the cart full of ammo. They then hear the British soldiers singing the same song, but in English.

The war is over. Japan lay in ruins. The Japanese soldiers surrender, uncertain if they will ever return home. But not all the Japanese stationed in Burma know this, and the British still fight those who are unwilling to surrender. They call on Mizushima to see if he can assist.

<blockquote><i>Can't you see that whatever you do is futile? The British have come, you Japanese have come, but war has changed nothing. Burma is still Burma. Burma is Buddha's country.</i></blockquote>

The film is one of the first two to show Japanese losses during WWII. Some soldiers refused to surrender at the end of the war, standing firm for their fallen comrades. This dedication to the fallen becomes a recurring theme, motivating characters to do things they do not want but feel are for the greater good.

As historical fiction, the film offers little substance. The film sees Japan's role in Burma as passive, while Japan's war crimes in the country and throughout the Pacific nations are well-documented. Yes, the film works at a small scale. Yes, the director may not have known about these atrocities. But the fact remains that this film is far more fiction than it intends.

That said, this film is more firmly anti-war than just about any American film about WWII that I've seen. Despite the US's best attempts to understand the war, its films are far too dedicated to spectacle and myths of heroism to make something this stark or bleak.

The film has a strong relationship to music, using diegetic music as something that transcends the screen. Sometimes, when the group sings a song, the words appear on the screen, as though inviting the audience to sing along. It is the force which can unify more than any violent action can.

While much of the film was shot in Japan, the filmmakers still traveled to Burma for some shots. And boy, is it worth it to see the gorgeous landscapes and stunning temples.

The film doesn't do quite as much for me as it has for others, but I can appreciate the tone and especially the anti-war messaging.

<blockquote><i>The soil of Burma is red, and so are its rocks.</i></blockquote>
