Because Kristoffer Borgli is not American, his take on a fundamentally American story is curious. By making Charlie an art curator, we explore this story through aesthetics, specifically how the characters relate to it. An art book that inexplicably lands on Charlie's desk at one point in the movie underscores the fundamental disconnect between the images we carry in our heads and the reality from which they are distilled.

As an image-driven culture, we carry movie meet-cutes around in our heads, or the idea that little white lies can lead to a blossoming romance. We put entire subcultures or racial minorities into buckets of understanding, much of it motivated by how it makes us appear to others. Most of our relationships are far more fragile than we realize, as only one dollop of contradictory information can shatter our illusion of who this person is. For whatever horrible things I may have done in my life, your thing is worse because I've spent years rationalizing my behavior to myself.

Alana Haim's character, Rachel, reminded me of someone I knew in art school whose moral compass put me in their crosshairs for reasons neither my professors nor I could figure out. We never interacted outside of class, had no mutual friends, but I was a monster they needed to call out. To their credit, I was a religious idiot who definitely had several problematic ideas swimming in my head. But I can safely say I wasn't the predator that they had constructed through the aesthetics of my work and how I presented myself.

Also, boys, please stop reading Freud and thinking that it is the same thing as therapy.
