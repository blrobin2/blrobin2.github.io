<b><a href="">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 7/52 | Wes Anderson's Top 10</a></b>

<blockquote><i>Madame de… was a very elegant, distinguished, and celebrated woman, seemingly destined to a delightful, uncomplicated existence. Probably nothing would have happened had it not been for those jewels…</i></blockquote>

A dazzling display of jewels lay open. Wardrobes, trunks, and closets filled with immaculate dresses, furs, and accessories line the room. The Countess, Louise, settles on the heart-shaped diamond earrings her husband, Count André, gave her as a wedding present. She stows them in her handbag and sneaks away. While out, Louise stops by the church to pray to the saints that the jeweler will buy the earrings.

Upon meeting with Rémy, the jeweler, she swears him to secrecy before revealing that her lavish spending has led to an enormous debt, which she hopes the sold jewels will help pay off. And who better to appraise the earrings than the man from whom André bought them? Rémy is reticent to purchase them back, but eventually agrees. At the opera that night, Louise pretends to have lost the earrings. André, dressed in his French Army general regalia, humors her and looks everywhere, unable to find them.

The following day, Jérôme, Rémy's son, sees a news article about a robbery at the opera. Rémy takes the article and the jewels directly to André, afraid of what the gossip about the robbery may do to his business. Explaining the circumstances, André accepts them without any fuss. He then takes them to his 8:00 appointment with his mistress, Lola, whom he sends off at the train station as she leaves for Constantinople. The earrings are a parting gift.

And so begins the journey that Louise's earrings take, and the affairs of Louise and André that help move them about in this tragedy of coincidence.

<blockquote><i>Coincidence is only so extraordinary because it's so natural.</i></blockquote>

It becomes clear early on that, while Louise and André care for one another, they are not passionately in love. Perhaps they were at one point. But now, both are aware of each other's suitors and mistresses, content to share their opulence and decorum. They sleep in separate bedrooms and lead their separate lives. But that changes when Louise starts falling for one of them, and André realizes he cares more than he thought.

<blockquote><i>Believe me, she'll find you very entertaining. But I'm warning you: She's an incorrigible flirt. She's an expert in dashing men's hopes. You know, torture through hope.</i></blockquote>

The humor of the film comes from the secrets kept between Louise and André, and more importantly, the way that those secrets come back to them. There are also small observational humor from the corners of their decadent life — an usher tired by standing and opening doors for André, or the superstitious nanny who drew a four of diamonds and two black sevens (meaning great loss). There's also a running gag: we never learn Louise and André's last names, always obscured by a sound or a camera angle (hence the title).

The cinematography moves like a dancer across the floor — elegant and fluid but never calling attention to itself. It's no surprise that the staunch aesthete Stanley Kubrick considered this his favorite movie. The experience is an endless visual reward of carefully-chosen compositions and eloquent blocking. It helps that the camera has so many beautiful sets and costumes to observe. The film received an Oscar nomination for Best Costume Design.

The humor falls away in pieces, leading to a melodrama of double standards—a dangerous game for a life lived so publicly. Louise is a victim of her desires only because she is a woman. The Count can have his affairs with imprudence because he believes that none of his affairs are motivated by love. Neither is in the right, and like any great melodrama, neither can have what they truly want. Both learn that what you cannot part with is what defines you.
