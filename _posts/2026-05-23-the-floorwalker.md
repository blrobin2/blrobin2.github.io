<i>(This review is written through the lens of Rudolf Arnheim's film theory and does not reflect my personal view on the film)</i>

To begin, we should establish what I mean by "realistic." While many of the visual gags in the film are exaggerated and heightened to a point where no one would consider them likely to occur in real life, they are realistic in the sense that they could be reproduced on stage in front of a live audience. Such is the nature of vaudeville, to which Chaplin is deeply indebted in his comedic tendencies.

For a film to have artistic merit, it must seek to do something with the medium that isn't possible on the stage. While this film has the benefit of multiple takes, montage, and composition, so long as they serve to maintain the illusion of continuity and stage-like presence, the film lacks artistic integrity. The camera remains squared with the backdrop, creating little more than subsections of the stage. With few exceptions, it remains static, as an audience member might be.

As entertainment, the film is amusing, but by failing to engage with the limitations and material qualities of film itself, it achieves nothing artistic.
