<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail" rel="nofollow">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 6/52 | Made in Spain</a></b>

<b>Somewhere on the Castilian plateau, around 1940…</b>

A mobile cinema enters the remote Spanish village of Hoyuelos. Children parade around the truck in excitement. The men push the kids away as they unload the equipment. A town crier announces that, at the town hall that night, there will be a special showing of <i>Frankenstein</i>. That evening, the townspeople bring their chairs from home and line them up in front of the screen, the children claiming the front row. As the film starts, the audience watches with enraptured attention.

Fernando sprays pesticides on his beehives. He carefully removes a frame and examines the bees. His wife, Teresa, writes a letter to a faraway lover with whom she parted during the Spanish Civil War. She doesn't know if he's still alive, but writes him all the same. After writing the letter, she rides her bike down the hill from the manor and into town. A train pulls into the station, and Teresa slips the letter into the mail bin attached to the train.

In the theater, young Ana and her older sister, Isabel, watch as the little girl encounters Frankenstein's monster. Ana is glued to the screen as the monster accepts the little girl's flower. Later, she is perplexed as the girl's father carries her lifeless body into the town square, unsure why they would kill her.

That night, after saying their prayers and going to bed, Ana lights a candle and asks Isabel why they killed the girl and the monster. Isabel tells Ana that they didn't kill the girl or the monster — that not only is the movie fake, but that Isabel claims to have seen the monster alive, living near the village as a spirit that people cannot see. If you make friends with the monster, you can talk to him whenever you want.

The film follows the family as they fixate on something outside of themselves in an effort to make meaning out of their lives.

<blockquote><i>Now neither malice nor hatred, nor even the fear of change — I only feel thirst, a thirst for I know not what. Rivers of life, where have you gone? Air, I need air. What do you see in the darkness that makes you silently tremble? I see not but only stare like a blind man facing directly into the sun. I shall fall where the fallen never rise.</i> — Joaquim Rubió i Ors, <i>El Libro De Las Niñas</i></blockquote>

Fernando is obsessed with beehives, writing about them, thinking about them, tending to them. In the bees' spiritually empty cycles of labor and death, he feels a deep sadness and horror. At this point in history, Franco's civil war overthrew the leftist government, silencing society under a fascist dictatorship. The people, like the bees, toil in meaningless order.

Through Teresa's letters, we experience her sense of isolation, disconnected from the rest of Spain in this small, remote village. Her longing for this distant lover is her longing to know what is actually happening in the world. The manor is, itself, a beehive, containing her and alienating her from meaningful interaction.

<blockquote><i>It's difficult to feel nostalgia after what we've been through these past few years. But sometimes, when I look around me and see so much loss, so much destruction, and so much sadness, something tells me perhaps our ability to really feel life has vanished along with all the rest.</i></blockquote>

Ana finds meaning in her desire to meet and befriend Frankenstein's monster. Isabel, knowingly lying about this monster, is more than happy to play along and mess with her sister. Later, we see that Isabel's disregard takes on more unsettling connotations. In one moment, their father teaches them about assorted mushrooms, which ones are safe to eat and which are dangerous. Ana finds one that smells delicious, but her father warns her of its toxicity and stomps on it, taking another life in front of her.

Part of this, too, is director Victor Erice reflecting on his childhood, drawing on moments he remembered from this period and on how it felt to be a child in Spain at that time. He saw the way isolation breeds restlessness in children — safe from harm, safe from thought. It's the rural kids who get bored and do something stupid. I grew up in a rural town, where kids robbed stores, broke into people's houses, became religious, and more, all at very young ages. With no outlet, darkness grows.

This film is the cornerstone that makes Guillermo del Toro's filmography make so much more sense. Films like <a href="https://boxd.it/4mVEh1" rel="nofollow"><i>The Devil's Backbone</i></a> and <i>Pan's Labyrinth</i> draw on similar themes of childhood imagination, creating a magical space amid political upheaval. What makes his movies different from this one, however, is del Toro's inability to tackle complex themes and symbolism.

Here, this subtlety was a requisite to get it past the Francoist state censor's office, where they assumed audiences would not be interested in something this "arty." Most of the film is spent observing the characters as they go through their lives. We seldom see Fernando, Teresa, or the girls in the same shot — they live their individual, isolated lives. When they are the same shot, they often occupy different planes, together yet distinct.

One of the most quietly groundbreaking aspects of this movie is that it is one of the first Spanish films to use actual children's voices instead of dubbing them with adults. The magic of children's laughter and the stomach-churning of their terrified screams really add a layer to the experience.
