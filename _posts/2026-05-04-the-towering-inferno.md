<b><a rel="nofollow">Cult Movie Challenge 2019 | 13/52 | 70s Disaster</a></b>

<b>I never thought I'd see Fred Astaire and explosions in the same movie, but life holds many such delights.</b>

A helicopter flies through Muir Woods, over San Fransisco Bay and the Golden Gate Bridge, and into the Financial District. But what's this? The 138-story Glass Tower is the world's tallest building. The helicopter lands on top, and the architect, Doug, exits to meet with the developer, James. James wants to build more skyscrapers like The Glass Tower all over the country. Tonight, they will have a dedication party on the top floor.

In the main utility room, Callahan, head chief of building utilities, runs a test with Wes, his assistant, frying a main circuit. Up on the 81st floor, an electrical short circuit starts a fire in a breaker box, inconveniently located next to highly flammable paint and rags. Harry, the chief security officer, sees a notice on the computer indicating that the sensor has detected a fire in the main utility room. The sensor, however, didn't trip the fire alarm, and on the cameras, everything looks okay.

Doug inspects the wiring and discovers that the electrical subcontractor cut some corners. He and James confront Duncan, whose son-in-law did the electrical work. Will, the electrical engineer, drops that they haven't even finished installing all the safety measures, and worries about having the party. Still, after a trillion other character beats and famous actors giving each other clandestine stage-kisses, the party begins. Meanwhile, the fire grows ever larger.

The film is a rare collaboration between Fox and Warner Bros., as both were working on a high-rise thriller, and producer Irwin Allen didn't want one to cannibalize the other. Because of Allen's success with <i>The Poseidon Adventure</i>, he had enough sway to make the collaboration happen. Similarly, both Steve McQueen and Paul Newman wanted top billing, so they shared equal billing in the credits.

The film contains all the requisite elements: an unnecessarily stacked cast, an exceptionally long runtime, and too little story to justify it. If the formula feels familiar here, it's in large part due to Stirling Silliphant reprising his role as screenwriter for <i>The Poseidon Adventure</i> and doing little more than changing the window dressing. Because the cast is more stacked than Poseidon, the first act of meeting all the players takes even longer, contributing to the near 3-hour runtime.

The movie has its moments! Mostly it's the explosions and people catching fire in wild ways. But the special effects hold up! We have disaster movies made within the past decade for more money that don't hit half as hard as this one. They invested in the stunts, and it pays off. I genuinely love the final set piece and its build-up.

I love how deeply 70s the interior design is — orange and dark wood for days. It reminds me of my grandparents' old house. Paul Newman does his best Jimmy Stewart-esque everyman. He and Steve McQueen look too similar — thank goodness they're wearing different clothes. While the women don't get much to do, Jennifer Jones gets to be part of the action more than anyone — not bad for an actress of 55 at the time.

This film is often considered the pinnacle of the 70s disaster wave. Having seen a dozen or so myself, I can say this one has more going for it than most. It's clear the influence this one had on the 90s disaster resurgence — move some cameras and update the cast, and it could double feature with <i>Dante's Peak</i> or <i>Daylight</i>. While it never achieves the tension of your <a href="https://boxd.it/546xE7" rel="nofollow"><i>Andromeda Strain</i></a> or <a href="https://boxd.it/5E7tP7" title="Runaway Train"><i>Runaway Train</i></a>, it sure as hell does more with its premise than <a href="https://boxd.it/9dJIbB" rel="nofollow"><i>Earthquake</i></a> or <a href="https://boxd.it/7REG" rel="nofollow"><i>Superdome</i></a>.
