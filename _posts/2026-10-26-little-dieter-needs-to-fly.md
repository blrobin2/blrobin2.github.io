<blockquote><i>And in those days shall men seek death, and shall not find it, and shall desire to die, and death shall flee from them.</i> — Revelation 9:6</blockquote>

Dieter Dengler enters Ed Hardy's Tattoo City, where he discusses a massive tattoo idea: a vision of the doors of heaven opening, with Death driving a horse-drawn chariot through fire and lightning. He relates it to a vision he had while being a Vietnam War POW, which helped him realize that Death didn't want him.

While driving his car down the highway, Dieter talks about an experience where the voices of his dead friends from the war call out to him. Dieter was shot down over Laos in 1966. He never wanted to go to war, but only to fulfill one desire: to fly. He did not realize the impact on the people below until he became their prisoner.

At the door of his SF home, Dieter repeatedly opens and closes it, reliving the freedom he was denied as a prisoner. His foyer is covered with paintings of open doors. His house is always packed with food, including thousands of pounds of rice and hundreds of pounds of wheat stored beneath the house.

The film tells of Dieter's capture and repeated escape attempts. In true Herzog fashion, he invites Dieter to relive some of those experiences, hiring Laotian actors to play his captors.

The film reflects on the psychological effects of traumatic experiences, such as war. They can live seemingly normal lives, but their inner lives tell a different story. Dieter is truly haunted: visions of the dead live with him and guide him.  The world he grew up in made no sense. The world he experienced in Laos was unbelievably grotesque.

Both Herzog and Dieter grew up in Germany and relay stories of the bombings during WWII. Witnessing one of those bombers look out of his plane over the city inspired Dieter to become a pilot. Because there were no opportunities in Germany, he sailed to the US.

Herzog has never hidden how he intentionally subverts the documentary form. Here, Herzog offers Dieter symbolic images and asks him to recount them as though they were his own. Scenes like the door opening and closing repeatedly were Herzog's idea, not Dieter's.

So we see how Herzog exorcises his demons through his experience with Dieter. Yes, Dieter did have all the horrific experiences he recounts and relives alongside Herzog. Yes, he had vivid hallucinations during and after his imprisonment. But several of the more poetic aspects of the storytelling come from Herzog's mind.
