<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 48/52 | Foreign-Language Oscar Winner</a></b>

Farm servant Ingeri starts a fire, waddling through the kitchen with her pregnant belly. Using a log, she opens the slat in the roof to let the smoke out. Clinging to the log, she prays for Odin to aid her. Meanwhile, farm owner Per Töre prays to the Christian God, and his wife, Märeta, prays alongside him for protection. Märeta pours hot wax on her wrists to symbolize Jesus' suffering.

Frida, another farm servant, chastises Ingeri for staying out all night. Märeta enters with candles for the church mass, handing the chore to Frida instead of Töre's daughter, Karin, who is sick. Töre and the farmhands sit down for breakfast. When he hears of Karin's sickness, he reminds his wife that a virgin must take the candles. Töre confesses she had a nightmare, but goes to fetch Karin.

While Ingeri makes lunch for Karin, she finds a toad in the kitchen. She hollows out the bread and puts the toad inside it. Karin dresses in fine silk clothes, delighting her mother. She asks Ingeri to come along, since she never gets out of the house. They must leave in a hurry, as the journey will take them all day. The two encounter men who seem to be the same ones Ingeri and Karin met last night at a party.

A raven watches the two women as they approach a riverside mill and bridge. Ingeri grows frightened of the dark woods and begs them to turn back. Karin asks the bridge keeper to watch Ingeri until she returns. Ingeri knows something, and it only becomes clear far too late.

<blockquote><i>See how the smoke trembles in the roof hole? As if whimpering and afraid. Yet it's only going out into the open air, where it has the whole sky to tumble about in. But it doesn't know that. […] People are the same way. They worry and tremble like leaves in a storm because of what they know and what they don't know.</i></blockquote>

After <i><a href="https://boxd.it/fbTUmr">The Seventh Seal</a></i> received slack for its historical accuracy, Bergman went ham and made this historically accurate nightmare. Screenwriter Ulla Isaksson, known for her historically accurate novels, wrote the screenplay. She brings in the juxtaposition of Norse religion and Christianity that was at play during Sweden's Middle Ages.

The film explores the guilt that Christianity has brought to Sweden. Ingeri prays to Odin for help—and he answers, but in a horrible way. Similarly, the younger brother of the two herdsmen, a boy, is the first to be truly racked with guilt, unable to eat without throwing up or convulsing, and scared to pray. Towards the end, we see guilt arise in Töre and Märeta as they try to take the blame for why all this has happened to them.

<blockquote><i>God alone knows where the guilt lies.</i></blockquote>

The bridge keeper in the movie matches descriptions of Odin from the period, but disheveled as though no longer at his peak. Odin sacrificed his left eye for wisdom—the bridge keeper also has one eye. He even has a high seat, not unlike Odin's Hliðskjálf, from where Ingeri has a vision of the three men who Karin will encounter. He has no name in these days, but he can hear and see what he wants to.

The SA and murder scene is so tense and horrifying. Though the film is not exploitative with nudity, it does not look away, except briefly to Ineri's eyes as she watches it happen. It's definitely the most graphic depiction of SA I've seen from this era, so be warned.

An iconic moment comes when Töre, in a fit of rage, rips a young birch tree down so he can perform a pagan tradition of cleansing himself with the branches—a practice still seen in Northern European saunas. It marks his turn away from the Christian God, trusting in something more fundamental to help him carry out his deed. But when he goes too far, he seeks repentance.

<blockquote><i>God, you saw it. […] You allowed it to happen! I don't understand you!</i></blockquote>

Cinematographer Sven Nykvist became Bergman's go-to cinematographer after things became strained between Bergman and Gunnar Fischer. Nykvist develops the poetic eye that marks so much of Bergman's work after this. His use of natural light and composition creates a deceptive simplicity that builds unspoken tension.

While this film is not as stirring or complex as <i>The Seventh Seal</i>, its poetic symbolism and careful cinematography elevate it beyond the exploitative elements in its story.
