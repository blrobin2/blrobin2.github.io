<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 11/52 | 1930s</a></b>

The canal barge L'Atalante sits parked near a village. Church bells ring. Juliette and Jean, newly married, exit and walk the village streets to the barge, which Jean captains. First mate Père Jules and the cabin boy play accordion and dance for the newlyweds. The newlyweds board the barge, riding off as Juliette's family solemnly watches them leave. Juliette and Jean try to make out, but a bunch of Père Jules' cats pounce on Jean.

Juliette has some difficulty adjusting to life on the river. One of Père Jules's cats gives birth to kittens on Juliette and Jean's bed. Juliette goes to grab new linens, only to find that Jean and the crew are not all that clean. So, Jean helps run a laundry line as Juliette asks the whole crew to bring in their soiled linens for cleaning. While Jean washes his hair in the basin, Juliette reminds him that if he opens his eyes underwater, he'll see the one he loves. Jean tries but cannot see her.

Jean and Juliette regularly fight as Jean becomes preoccupied with his work and Juliette seeks moments of reprieve. Juliette, upon learning that the crew is headed for Paris, listens to a radio program about the latest fashions and sews some clothes on her machine. Père Jules and Juliette become friends as Jules tries on some of Juliette's clothes and Jules entertains Juliette with many of the novelties he's picked up on his travels. Jean walks in on Jules and Juliette hanging out and grows jealous, throwing plates and causing a scene.

The film follows Jean and Juliette as they navigate their relationships with the crew and each other.

French pop culture in the 1930s was obsessed with gritty realism, especially the hard lives of riverfront workers. Of course, being a decade of economic collapse, fluffy escapism also filled the airwaves. Amidst this were the movies deemed "poetic realism," coming from those dreamy directors like René Clair. Jean Vigo's short, <i><a href=""https://boxd.it/4jzjof">Zero for Conduct</a></i>, leaned heavily into the surreal. At the same time, this movie, his only full release before his untimely death, is firmly rooted in realism, letting the absurdity of its side characters serve as the playful buoyancy that keeps it from being too heavy.

That isn't to say the film doesn't have its moments of magic. Once we enter Paris, we encounter fortune tellers, magicians, and singing peddlers. Michel Simon's Père Jules is where the movie is at its most playful, with his worldly collection of curios, tattoos, and silly cats that defy gravity and descend from anywhere. Boris Kaufman's cinematography is artistic and angular, especially when outdoors, while also maintaining a sort of vérité quality that keeps it from ever feeling too experimental.

This blend of levity and playfulness would become a major influence on the French New Wave. Also, astute viewers who have seen enough French New Wave will recognize locations in this movie that the later films would visit to pay homage.

If you're not looking for it, the magic of everyday life will pass you by. In the everyday work that keeps us alive, we may miss the love that keeps us afloat. When you see the one you love dancing underwater, it is not the world that has changed, but you and your ability to see what was there waiting for you.
