<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qaTwm/detail" rel="nofollow">Asian Cinema Challenge 2023 | 37/52 | Directed by Akira Kurosawa</a></b>

<b>Sengoku Period (1571) Japan</b>

Takeda Shingen, daimyō of the Kai province, sits with his brother, Nobukado, who bears a striking resemblance to Shingen. Both stare at a man who bears an uncanny resemblance to Shingen. Both Nobukado and the man are dressed exactly like Shingen, too. The man was going to be crucified for stealing, but Nobukado, who had acted as Shingen’s double for many years, thought the man would be useful as a double.

We cut to day twenty of the Takeda clan’s siege on the Tokugawa shogunate castle. A messenger brings word to Shingen’s retinue that they’ve cut off the castle's water supply. Except it is not Shingen sitting at the circle, but the thief, acting as kagemusha, or political decoy. They send a messenger to where Shingen awaits word of the cut aqueduct. Word also travels of a flute player inside the castle who plays every night. They predict that if the flute plays tonight, the garrison will hold — if not, the castle falls. Shingen is desperate to hear the flute and asks for a seat to be held for him at the castle.

Sure enough, that night, the flute plays. Then, an explosive shot. Word travels to Tokugawa Ieyasu, Oda Nobunga, and Uesugi Kenshin that a sniper made an attempt on Lord Shingen’s life, and that he was critically wounded. But they do not know if he still lives. The Takeda soldiers retreat from the castle—they can only surmise that Lord Shingen is dead, except that another soldier points to the horizon, over which rides Lord Shingen’s kagemusha. In secret, the dying Lord Shingen advises his generals to keep his death a secret for at least three years, and to give up on the lord’s desire to fly the Takeda flag in Kyoto. Instead, they must protect the clan, or else it will disappear. And the kagemusha will hold Shingen’s place.

The film follows the next few years and the consequences of this choice.

The film explores the nature of reputation — that is, what people believe is more important than what is. Shingen calls the kagemusha a scoundrel, and the kagemusha retorts that he merely stole a few coins, but Shingen has slaughtered hundreds. Shingen agrees that he, too, is a scoundrel. After all, he banished his father and killed his son. Outside of Shingen, other warlords respect his prowess, even if it has caused them tremendous pain. And the kagemusha holds an enormous amount of power so long as people believe he is Lord Shingen — without that belief, his reputation is worthless. But as a person, he may contain a gentleness that deserves a different sort of respect.

In today’s US political climate, where belief reigns over truth, we learn that our government’s most effective tool for control is its propaganda. As US citizens, our history, civics, and cultural education all flow through this machine, leading people to wild beliefs about how our country acts in the world. People think Trump is ruining our reputation overseas, when those who know history have never held the US in any esteem. Soldiers die, people vote, and families fracture over belief, not truth. So, simp for your imperial leader and see where it gets you.

The film is full of beautiful cinematography and composition. Its opening is so uncanny with the three Shingen all sitting together. The film employs an impressive double exposure, with Tatsuya Nakadai playing both Shingen and the kagemusha. This scene and others are shot as still, long takes, composed to look flat like classical Japanese art. This, combined with the striking use of color, makes for an aesthetically pleasing experience throughout, especially in the dream sequence and the fights. The film does a great job of making war incredibly unappealing. The victories are tiny, but the casualties and the frivolousness of all that death are immeasurable.

The film is quite long. The Criterion restoration restores 18 minutes of film previously cut. Included in those scenes is Takashi Shimura’s last collaboration with Kurosawa. It’s hard to say whether the film justifies its length, given the poetic way in which scenes stack up with similar reflections on the events. Like, my summary covers both the first 20 minutes and the first hour, just in terms of how the movie uses its time. That said, Kurosawa has never been one for brevity.

In the end, the movie feels like a take on The Prince and the Pauper, where the Pauper must take the prince’s place. It has the comedy of the kagemusha being “lower class” and having to learn how to present himself, as well as the breath-holding moments when something threatens to reveal his true identity, and he must think swiftly, showing that he is more like the man he is replacing than perhaps they give him credit for. By the end, I couldn’t deny how great this is.

Oh, and this movie has a white people jump scare! Just so you know.
