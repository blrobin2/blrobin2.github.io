<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail" rel="nofollow">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 8/52 | Romance</a></b>

<blockquote><i>Could there be anything more terrifying than a husband and wife who hate each other?</i> — August Strindberg</blockquote>

Marianne and Johan, a bourgeois couple, pose with their two daughters, Eva and Karin. A magazine interviews them after renewing their marriage contract on their 10th anniversary. Asking John about himself, he has a laundry list of what interests him, what he's good at, and his political beliefs. Meanwhile, Marianne can only think of herself as a wife and a mother. Still, the two appear happy in their life together.

Later, Marianne and Johan have dinner with Katarina and Peter, where Johan reads snippets from the magazine piece to entertain the table. Peter does not hide the fact that he and Katarina are unhappy with one another. Looking to change the subject, the men go to the study for coffee while the women stay behind and clean up dinner. But Peter, drunk and not slowing down, cannot get over how happy Marianne and Johan must be, and brings up his marriage's problems, leading to the two agreeing to a divorce.

That night, after Katarina and Peter leave, Johan and Marianne assure one another that their relationship will never be like that. They recognize that their privilege offers them luxuries that make their experience easier. The film follows the arc of Marianne and Johan's relationship through a series of vignettes that piece together their lives.

For <i>Fanny and Alexander</i>, I watched the full miniseries before watching the movie, and I found the movie a little lacking as a result. So, let's try the opposite and watch the movie first. The hope is that, when I see MORE footage, it will potentially improve the experience.

Because the movie was filmed for television, the cinematography is not as vibrant or refined as in other Bergman films. Cinematographer Sven Nykvist didn't know the TV show would be recut into a movie, so he would have made it more cinematic. Still, the grounded and fluid cinematography keeps the experience intimate, doing as little as possible to remind us that we are watching a movie.

The true ground of the movie, however, is Liv Ullmann and Erland Josephson's performances. They're on screen the entire time and must traverse through a wide spectrum of emotions, arguments, and moments. We see them at their most profound and their most pitiful. Johan has carried years of resentment in silence, while Marianne has been far too understanding and blissfully unaware of Johan's misery. Of course, it was never her job to force Johan to be honest — his resentment built from his own cowardice and inability to communicate.

It's hard not to compare the film with Berman's own relationship with Liv Ullmann, who plays Marianne. Bergman was 20 years older than Ullmann, just as Johan is to Paula. The film also references Johan's previous marriage to a pop star, not unlike Bergman's previous marriage to concert pianist Käbi Laretei. The characters feel real because they are rooted in something quite real. I really want to know how Liv Ullman handled continuing to be in Bergman movies, especially this one.

It's also hard not to see all the movies that this one has influenced. From <i>Annie Hall</i> to <a href="https://boxd.it/6txSRj" rel="nofollow"><i>Marriage Story</i></a>, this frank internal look at relationships explores love through conversation, episodes from their lives, and the complexity of figuring out what love can look like years down the line. This movie wasn't the first to do it, but the way folks did it afterward used this movie as a benchmark. For all the imitators, few capture the movie's authentic emotional resonance.
