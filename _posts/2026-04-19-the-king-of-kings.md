<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 10/52 | 1920s</a></b>

<b>Cecil B. DeMille said #FreePalestine.</b>

<blockquote><i>The events portrayed by this picture occurred in Palestine nineteen centuries ago, when the Jews were under the complete subjection of Rome — even their own High Priest being appointed by the Roman procurator. This is a story of JESUS of NAZARETH… He, Himself, commanded that His message be carried to the uttermost parts of the earth. May this portrayal play a reverent part in the spirit of that great command.</i></blockquote>

So reads the opening cards over a duo-tone Technicolor sunrise. We jump to Judea and meet the beautiful and irreverent courtesan, Mary Magdalene, entertaining a host of men at a decadent party, surrounded by leopards, swans, and zebras. But when a Bacchus-type asks for a kiss, she denies him, awaiting her lover, Judas. A party member reveals that he saw Judas with a band of beggars, led by a carpenter from Nazareth. One man claims he saw this carpenter heal a blind man — another says that he raised a man from the dead.

Judas joined the disciples and Jesus, believing that Jesus would be the next king. Jesus heals a young boy named Mark, who would later write one of the Gospels. The Pharisees hang around, annoyed by Mark's joy, but the giant named Peter stops them from hurting the boy. We meet John, James, Thomas, Andrew, Bartholomew, the other James, Phillip, Thaddeus, and Simon as they gather around Mary, the mother of Jesus, weaving and tending to doves. The sun sets, and the Sabbath begins. The Pharisees are excited over the idea of Jesus healing on the Sabbath and breaking the law.

Young Mark leads a blind girl to Mary's side as she begs to meet Jesus. She bows before someone, and the room grows quiet. The darkness falls away from her eyes in a pillar of light. As her eyes open, we finally see the pale face of a British man — the countenance of Jesus. Having healed the girl, Jesus sends her home to share the news of her healing. Judas is annoyed that Jesus is healing the poor instead of the rich. Mary Magdala arrives in a heap of fury, but stops short when she sees Jesus. He casts the Seven Deadly Sins from her, freeing her at last.

Will this young go-getter be the king that Judas expects him to be? Or will the envy of the Chief Priests and Scribes be the end of ol' Jesus?

Cecil B. DeMille was probably my first notion of a director, from cartoons regularly misquoting "I'm ready for my close-up, Mr. DeMille" from Sunset Boulevard, to the image of a man in high boots and riding breeches carrying a megaphone, pop culture supplanted him in my brain before I knew anything about him. In part, this is because he survived three generations of filmmaking, being once an innovator akin to D.W. Griffith, then later the prototype for the circus ringleader-as-director that Fellini "elevated." But he never had to change with the times.

Though the Bible never states that Mary Magdalene was a sex worker, and certainly never implies that she and Judas Iscariot were lovers, DeMille's imagining of her has become the de facto cultural understanding of the character. The Crucifixion is a showstopper, with lightning painting the sky and the earth swallowing up the onlookers. Screenwriter Jeanie Macpherson was not concerned with accuracy — she was concerned with telling a good story. To clarify, I don't care about the story's accuracy or whatever. In fact, I think this is an improvement on the traditional narrative.

The film never hides its then massive budget. Most outstanding is the duo-tone Technicolor, with its ruby reds and emerald greens, bringing to life sets nearly a century old. The filmmakers use it only for the opening and the (spoiler alert) resurrection, creating a juxtaposition between the lavishly wealthy and the despondently poor that is unified by Jesus' sacrifice. When the color returns, it fuckin rips. In some dialogue, the film uses quotes from the Bible, further suggesting the narrative's supposed Biblical accuracy.

What truly makes this movie outstanding are the compositions. The huge cast, decadent sets, and dazzling costumes create images akin to classical paintings. The multiple-exposure sequence in which the Seven Deadly Sins leave Mary Magdalene looks awesome. The budget for the doves rivals that of John Woo's movies. The film really pops off at the halfway point, when it transitions to Jesus' death. The camera following the cross as it drags along the stone streets is such a contemporary shot.

Yes, it's all sentimental nonsense, but it's a well-structured retelling, and it sure is pretty to look at.
