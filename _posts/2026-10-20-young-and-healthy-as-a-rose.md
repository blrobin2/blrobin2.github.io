Stiv drives down a street in Belgrade, a giant poster of then-president Josip Broz Tito hanging from a building. An announcement from the People's Youth and Socialist Union celebrates Tito's birthday. Tito speaks, encouraging the youth to build a strong Socialist country. Stiv leaves Belgrade and drives into the country. Stiv used to be a member of the youth brigade but has turned to crime.

At a gas station, Stiv uses a dead body as a distraction so that he can steal an old couple's car. He picks up a couple of hitchhikers, but they immediately hold him up. He gets the upper hand and uses their gun to drive them out to a field, where he shoots them in the back. Back in Belgrade, he flirts with several different women before hooking up with one and borrowing money.

The film follows Stiv's perspective, jumping around in time as his celebrity grows. Will Stiv pay for his crimes?

The film is shot almost entirely on handheld cameras, unconcerned with stable shots or clean framing. The filmmakers often shoot from cars, including during Stiv's chases. The footage will speed up to create an intense energy reminiscent of early French New Wave films like <i>Shoot the Piano Player</i>.

The Yugoslav Black Wave movement drew inspiration from the French New Wave, Czech New Wave, and the 1968 student demonstrations in Yugoslavia. The movement sought greater artistic freedom and aimed to critique certain Socialist policies. Several films were banned, with filmmakers forced to leave the country. This film was not, but it wasn't shown publicly for 35 years after its release.

Stiv, played by Dragan Nikolić, narrates throughout and often addresses the camera, sometimes from other cars. He constantly switches to new stolen cars. He prides himself on being a waste of talent. Dragan Nikoliċ reads like a Yugoslav Jean-Paul Belmondo.

By this point in history, Stalin had already moved the USSR towards totalitarian control. The movie suggests Yugoslavia's police force was corrupt, but the filmmakers had no definitive proof. It wasn't until the 90s that sources surfaced about collaboration between the Yugoslav secret service and organized crime.

I appreciate the film's confrontational, intense energy. Of all the "rebellious" movies I've seen, this one feels it, especially as things escalate in the third act. I don't know whether the Yugoslav Black Wave movement motivated me to download this movie in college, but I'm glad I have a copy, and I look forward to watching more movies from the period.
