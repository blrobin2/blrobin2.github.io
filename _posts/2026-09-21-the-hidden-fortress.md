Tahei and Matashichi, dressed in rags and haggard, cross the land, yelling at one another. They thought they'd make money by being in the war, so they sold their homes. Instead, the Akizuki enlisted them as gravediggers. Two days ago, they escaped with no food, let alone no fortune. Their quarrel continues, and the two go their separate ways.

Tahei comes across a Yamana checkpoint, where soldiers warn him not to cross. Tahei gets lost in the fog, accidentally crosses the checkpoint, and is captured. Matashichi enters a town and finds a notice offering 10 ryo for the return of Princess Yuki of Akizuki to the Lord of Yamana. The Yamana catch up and capture Matashichi, as well as several other men in the town.

The two reunite at the prisoner camp, digging through the Akizuki castle ruins. A prison riot breaks out. The men slip away, find some rice, and make a camp out in the wilderness. As they work out their plan, Matashichi suggests finding Princess Yuki for the reward. Tahei is too afraid of failing, but they cannot cross the border. In the water, they spot a piece of Akizuki gold hidden in a tree branch.

Realizing the prison camp was digging up the Akizuki reserve gold, the two set out to find more. Along the way, they encounter a mysterious man who may prove more helpful than they realize.

Finally, I check out the Kurosawa film that influenced George Lucas in the making of <i>Star Wars</i>. From the first-act synopsis alone, I'm sure fans can see several parallels. Beyond <i>Star Wars</i>, this film helped establish the romantic-adventure template so many fantasy films followed: the sarcastic hero, the comedic support, the "damsel in distress" who is more heroic and capable than the comedic support, the wise sage, and so on.

That's where it's hard for me to assess this movie. I'm so accustomed to the formula that seeing it played so straight is difficult to get excited about. It's not a template I particularly love, but it works quite well here. Tahei and Matashichi are quite silly and get into way more hijinks than you might expect, giving the movie an overall lighter tone than you might expect from Kurosawa.

Mifune is fantastic, as always, as General Makabe Rokurōta. Misa Uehara plays badass well — it's a shame she only acted for a couple more years before giving it up. And of course I'm always happy to see Takashi Shimura. Nothing in the script demands too much of them, but they give the material the intensity it needs.

The film is most interesting when Princess Yuki encounters the Fire Festival worshippers, who give her a new perspective on how short and petty life can be. Through this philosophy, she sees the world for all its beauty and all its ugliness. Moreover, the perspective takes away her fear of death and gives her a new resolve.

Overall, this film might appeal more to adventure-oriented viewers. Still, Ichio Yamazaki's widescreen cinematography (the first of Kurosawa's films to feature this format) and the film's philosophical underpinnings make it a wonderful watch all the same.
