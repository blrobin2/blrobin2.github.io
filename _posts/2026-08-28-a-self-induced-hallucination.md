<blockquote><i>I'm talking to my computer. It's five in the morning. I'm totally alone. I'm talking to my computer.</i></blockquote>

Jane Schoenbrun's found-footage documentary on Slender Man offers a fascinating and often hilarious look at how creepypasta spread on the internet and the real-world ramifications of refusing to admit the story is just a story. Like <i>Ghostwatch</i> or <i>The Blair Witch Project</i>, creepypasta attempted to dupe its children and young adult audience into believing that the stories contained some kernel of truth.

<blockquote><i>We forget how much it sucks to be a kid.</i></blockquote>

For most folks, Slenderman was dumb fun. But for a few, it was real enough to warrant harming friends, family, or themselves. On the other side, some refuse to believe any of it, including the news stories about the stabbings, calling parents and suspects "crisis actors."

<blockquote><i>Your children are becoming possessed through the films, books, and video games that are a steady diet of occult, sorcery, demonism, satanism, and shamanism.</i></blockquote>

This approach to blending the imagined and the real through the lens of a webcam provides a foundation for the mentality behind <a href="https://boxd.it/3bYNAZ"><i>We're All Going to the World's Fair</i></a>. Several moments from this doc were recreated for <i>World's Fair</i>. In all of Schoenburn's work that I've seen, they deal with this blur between consumed media and how it distorts our perception of reality.
