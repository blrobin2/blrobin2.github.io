Jonas lays a garland of flowers on his mother's grave.

Magit hides behind a boulder. Katla, Magit's sister, shows Magit a body bound and drowned in the river — the people of their village had stoned Katla and Magit's mother before burning her.

They will run to somewhere where Katla can find a husband, whom she will bind to her with witchcraft. So long as they tell no one, and no one knows them, they will be safe.

They find a boy named Johann who falls for Katla. His son, Jonas, doesn't take to Katla, throwing a wrench in her plan. Meanwhile, Magit may or may not be having visions.

The gorgeous monochrome cinematography makes Iceland look desolate and uninviting. The compositions are immaculate — some of the most beautiful frames I've seen in a movie. The movie is patient, but far from slow — quiet and reflective with an air of folklore.

The atmosphere is bleak, taking the Grimm Fairy Tale and planting it in a world where fairy tales become a means of coping. The witchcraft in the movie feels both real and entirely fictional, and the film never gives us anything definitive.

By entering the world through Magit, we experience her subjectivity, leading to the most striking moments of the film. Björk is incredible as Magit, giving her a sincerity that holds the whole film together. I love her shadow puppets that make the sounds of their respective animals!

I don't know, man, this one crept up on me! I like it more the more I think about it.
