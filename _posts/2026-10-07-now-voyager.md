To Mrs. Vale chagrin, her daughter, Lisa, comes home with Dr. Jaquith so that he may evaluate Lisa's sister, Charlotte, and her mental health. Charlotte catches on and anxiously tries to leave, but the doctor follows her to her room. There, she has ivory carvings that the doctor admires. She confides in him stories of her mother's controlling nature.

Dr. Jaquith returns downstairs and chastises Mrs. Vale. Lisa's daughter, June, comes home, fashionable and nosy. She rags Charlotte, triggering a nervous breakdown. The doctor recommends a few weeks at his sanitarium. After a few weeks, Lisa comes to check on her. Charlotte could go home, but Lisa has arranged a cruise for her.

She joins the cruise as Miss Beauchamp, taking the last-minute place of the actual Miss Beauchamp. The captain asks her to join a cabin with another lone traveler, Jerry Durrance, who wishes to be a regular tourist. While out, he telegrams his wife, Isobel. Charlotte confesses her true name. Her air of mystery and demeanor holds Jerry's attention.

The film follows chapters of Charlotte's life as she discovers her independence and happiness.

<blockquote><i>The untold want by life and land ne'er granted,<br>
Now, voyager, sail thou forth, to seek and find.<br>
</i>— Walt Whitman</blockquote>

The film explores how our upbringing shapes our sense of self—how mothers who don't want their daughters can convince those daughters that something is fundamentally unlovable about them. And so, they turn inwards, afraid of burdening the rest of the world and not trusting any kind words in their direction. In that way, it also looks at all the ways we can be alone in the world. Being single is one type of loneliness, but being married and not in love is quite another.

Mrs. Vale considers Charlotte her ugly duckling, incapable of making her own choices. She does everything in her power to control Charlotte. When Charlotte first falls in love with a captain at 20, her mother forbids it. She shuts Charlotte off from the world, hoping to create a nurse who lives only to tend to her needs. But over the film, Charlotte discovers a courage that faces the bark and stares down the bite.

Bette Davis' journey as Charlotte is so beautiful. We watch the insecurities fall away and the sense of self outside of her mother, outside of any man, blossom. Like many of us, her sense of what people do comes from books she's read, but it grows into something more authentic as she develops deeper connections to the world. It starts with some borrowed butterfly wings, a shawl left by the real Miss Beauchamp.

The film is a stirring, complicated melodrama that brings up ideas of mental health, chosen family, and healthy boundaries. I felt fiercely protective of Charlotte, and my heart swelled when she found what gives her life meaning.
