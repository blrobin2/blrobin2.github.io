<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 4/52 | Horror</a></b>

A group of women, led by Carmen Monteverde, prays for protection. In another room, men gather to prep the elderly General Enrique Monteverde for his trial. One worries that, if the General goes down, they'll go down with him. Everyone leaves except the General, his wife Carmen, his daughter Natalia, his granddaughter Sara, and their security guard Letona. Oh, and the handful of Kaqchikel servants. One servant woman, Valeriana, prays for Don Enrique and his family. Sara asks her mother, Natalia, why people on the Internet say such mean things about her grandfather.

While Enrique sleeps, he hears a woman crying. Startled awake, he slips out of bed, grabs one of his many guns from the closet, and follows the crying downstairs. He looks into a dark room when a figure approaches behind him. He turns to shoot, narrowly missing Carmen's head. Waking everyone else, Letona charges in to take the gun as Enrique orders a search of the house for a guerrilla spy. No one heard the woman crying except Enrique. One servant says that it's "her" in her native tongue.

The following morning, all the servants ask to leave the house. Carmen says they are ungrateful and that no one will hire them—all leave except for Valeriana. At the trial, an indigenous woman gives an account of the General's army destroying their homes, their crops, and systematically raping the women and murdering the men and children. According to the trial's defense, no civilians were harmed, only armed guerrilla members. That is, the General did not commit the genocide they accuse him of.

<blockquote><i>People are to the guerrilla as water to fish: if you have to get rid of the fish, first you have to get rid of the water.</i></blockquote>

The trial finds him guilty, but the Constitutional Court annuls the trial, saying there is insufficient evidence. Protests form outside their home. Valeriana brings in a woman named Alma to assist her in serving the house. The film follows the aftermath of the court trial.

Enrique Monteverde is based on the real-life dictator Efraín Ríos Montt, who served as the President of Guatemala from 1982 to 1983. This was during the Guatemalan Civil War, which lasted from 1960 to 1996. We know for certain now that the CIA helped back his brief tenure, during which they funded the genocide of the indigenous Mayan people, leading to the death 1/3 of the Mayan-Ixil and Mayan-Kaqchickel people, 38% who were children under twelve. All this was done in the name of "weakening the Marxist guerrillas."

By taking the perspective of the Monteverde family, we see the justifications they have swallowed in the years since the genocide. Carmen tries to convince Natalia that the many women who gave testimony were all paid sex workers and communist liars. Or that the country must move on if it is to recover from this tragedy. Natalia is the pivot, concerned only with the truth — we learn the details as she does, and we must come to grips with the outcome.

The film uses water as a recurring motif. The faucets in the house seem to turn on all by themselves. Sara fixates on learning to hold her breath underwater. The title "La Llorona" loosely translates to "The Weeping Woman." Alma seems to have a strong relationship with water, as it is such an important facet of her culture.

The film has a methodical, atmospheric pace. Traditional horror fans, even those who like slow burns, may find their patience tested. But if you come in expecting a traditional horror movie, you will be disappointed. While the film contains horror elements, it is about catharsis — the prayer that vile people like Enrique Monteverde are haunted by their actions, especially in a culture that is uninterested in prosecuting the guilty if they hold enough power and are white-passing.
