After watching <a href="https://boxd.it/fzIU8t" rel="nofollow"><i>The Blood of a Poet</i></a>, I decided to revisit this one, which I haven't seen since college. I anticipated that the film was difficult in some way that prevented me from scoring it higher. Still, it’s almost the opposite — the film is so beholden to the original fairy tale that, in its simplicity, it loses the opportunity to connect us with the characters.

That said, the film is beautiful to look at — the costumes, the surreal castle, and the sense of wonder that Cocteau's special effects evoke compel the film in ways the story does not. It's also fascinating to watch so soon after <i>The Blood of a Poet</i>, seeing the various visual tricks he used there applied to this story. I also feel like some of the visual language surrounding the house is reminiscent of the 20s version of <a href="https://boxd.it/bsnWHN" rel="nofollow"><i>The Fall of the House of Usher</i></a>.

Another facet that I feel comes through in both <i>Blood</i> and this is Cocteau's queerness. He films the men in his stories with such an adoring lens. Here, Jean Marais, who plays The Beast and Avenant, seems almost the filmmaker's wish fulfillment more than Belle's. The Beast must hide his love, trusting it only to someone who might understand him — all others would rather murder or rob him.

While I wish I loved it more, I still appreciate Cocteau's dedication to bringing the classic fairytale to life in a way that feels authentic to his sensibility.
