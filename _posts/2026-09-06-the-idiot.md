In a train car, passengers lay sleeping. A scream awakens them. Kameda dreamed about getting shot — a recurring nightmare of his, as he was a war criminal sentenced to death, only for his sentence to be commuted after they realized he wasn't the war criminal. He explains to another passenger, Akama, about his epileptic dementia — he has had so many episodes that he suffers from severe brain damage. Kameda is on his way to see his only living relative, Ono.

They stop in Sapporo, where Akama shows Kameda a picture of Taeko Nasu, a woman who served as mistress to Tohata, a rich man, before running away. Akama has bought her a ring, but doesn't know that Tohata has offered a man named Kayama a substantial dowry to marry Taeko. Kameda feels deep sorrow in the picture, which makes him cry. The two go their separate ways.

Ono's family tries to decide what to do with Akama. They decide that he can rent a room from Kayama, who they expect to be well off after the dowry. Kameda receives an inheritance from his now-deceased father. With that money, perhaps he can top the dowry and get Taeko for himself.

And so, circumstances bring both Akama and Kameda together under the shadow of Taeko. What will happen to their friendship? And what of Taeko, whom men seem to trade around like property? Can anyone come out happy? It's based on Dostoevsky, so what do you think?

The film uses extensive interstitials in the first act to fill in gaps—in part because the studio cut the original 2-part version into a single film. The film's reliance on these interstitials to provide background is overwhelming and makes the story that much harder to follow. The original was more faithful to the novel, but the cuts cause the film to drift.

That said, once we get into the story, the performances are wonderful. The visual storytelling does so much with few exchanged words. Some of the visual motifs are so intense that I couldn't help but laugh. But the emotions and tension really crept up on me, and the whole experience still weighs on me.

Kameda, in his simple sincerity, becomes everyone's scapegoat, assuming malicious intent because their intentions are so gray and compromised. Akama's mother is the happiest character because she's the most oblivious, as if she lost her mind somewhere along the way.

It's no surprise that, with the title, the movie leads us to believe Kameda is the idiot—he himself and everyone else call him that. But, of course, everyone else is the idiot, making ruinous choices, believing in their own martyrdom.

I once heard Herzog say, regarding mental health, that there are corners of our house that should remain unswept, cobwebs untouched, for our minds are not ready to see what lies beneath them. Whether I agree or not, I feel like this movie supports that notion — if you are happy, do not chase misery in the attempt to be noble.

Time to put on something light and floofy.
