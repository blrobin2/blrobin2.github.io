A train rushes down the tracks. John, dressed in black with a chain belt, slips quietly through a compartment door, slowly to avoid notice of a boy at the windows. John stops himself. A whipping sound and a set of stairs from his childhood flood his senses. The boy looks into the window reflection and sees John using a tool to open one of the rooms. Inside, a newlywed couple embraces one another. John raises a meat cleaver. The screech and whistle of the train drowns out the screams. Childhood flashbacks fill John's head. Then, the dead bride, and John wiping down his cleaver on the veil.

John hones his razor blade before shaving. His wife, Mildred, calls for him. He announces to us that he is a serial killer who has killed five young women, all while he completes his beauty routine. He catches a fly, looking as though he may set it free, but instead feeds it to his parrot. Visions from his childhood only become clearer when he kills — he will not stop until he finds out the whole truth. Mildred, his wife, seems to despise him. Still, John asks for a divorce, and she refuses. Though he inherited his family's bridal dress factory, it is Mildred's wealth that supports it.

At work, a model named Helen applies for a vacant spot after a model who mysteriously disappeared a couple of weeks ago. Mildred drags John to a seance to contact Mildred's first husband. A detective is snooping around, questioning John about the recent murders, all women who worked for John, but no hard evidence points to him. One of the models who works for John tells him that she is to be married and must quit, so he invites her over to see his bridal gown museum dedicated to his mother's memory. He allows her to pick out a dress.

<blockquote><i>A woman should live only until her wedding night, love once, then die.</i></blockquote>

The film has a bit of an uneven pace, letting scenes linger in their visual splendor. Mario Bava knows how to shoot a scene — in this movie, he puts such intense effort into each frame. The Eastmancolor looks tremendous, and the lighting is immaculate. The atmosphere gets increasingly bizarre.

I wish I could say I was the first to notice the parallels between this movie and <i>American Psycho</i>, but I suppose the similarities are too glaring to ignore. The internal monologue, the beauty routine, and the full conviction that they are insane and too smart for anyone to notice. It also comes up in the plot, but I don't want to spoil anything.

Giallo's always putting Burt Bacharach-ass ballads over the most grotesque murders. I love how grandiose the score is, haha.

Laura Betti is having a blast. She's fully aware of the absurdity of her character, an intentional choice when Betti asked to be in Bava's next movie. She had just won the Volpi Cup for <i>Teorema</i> and wanted to do something "anti-intellectual." You can tell Bava didn't give much to Stephen Forsyth, but it's nothing terrible.

Overall, this film is better than advertised, but understandably not among the "great" Bava films.
