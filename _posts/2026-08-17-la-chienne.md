<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 30/52 | Black and White</b></b>

Through a hand puppet theater, the puppets argue and fight over whether the movie we're about to watch is a comedy or a drama. A soldier puppet enters, assuring the audience that the story is neither a comedy nor a drama, contains no morals, and has nothing to prove. We meet "He", also known as Maurice, an older man whose shyness and naivety lead people to believe that he is unintelligent. "She" is Lulu, a charming yet vulgar woman who lies with complete sincerity. "The Other Guy" is just Déde.

Henroit & Company, a hosiery wholesaler, holds a company celebration to honor the boss for an award he received. The other men gather around Maurice to pressure him to come out drinking with them, but Maurice insists on returning home to his wife, Adèle. On the streets, a drunken Déde chastises Lulu, demanding money from her. As Maurice passes, he sees Déde hit Lulu, and Maurice pops him. Lulu confesses that she has no money for a taxi, but must take Déde with her. Maurice calls a cab to take Déde home.

Maurice then walks Lulu home and learns that Déde has been Lulu's pimp for three years. During the walk, Maurice claims to be a painter. He wishes to see her again, and she tells him that she will write. Once Maurice arrives home, he stumbles over his easel and canvas, accidentally awakening Adèle, who is furious at him for being out so late. She also complains about his paintings and how they clutter up the apartment, saying that his passion for painting has made him a laughingstock.

One month later, Maurice has moved Lulu out of her place with her family and into an apartment he decorated with his paintings. He has also taken her as his mistress, even though Lulu isn't attracted to him. Déde is thrilled that Lulu has found a steady income. Little does he know that Maurice's money comes from Adèle. The film follows the complicated relationship between Maurice, Lulu, and Déde.

As the opening states, none of the leads are heroes or villains. They are each wretched in their own way. The film's poetic justice has a certain nihilism to it: the punishment doesn't inherently fit the crime, but everyone who touches this circle of people suffers.

The film was Renoir's second sound film. He never found much success during the silent era. By insisting on direct sound, his crew created a type of realism and texture heretofore unseen in talkies.

The film also makes excellent use of interior spaces, letting them become extensions of the characters, their relationships to money. The cinematography focuses on long takes and depth of field, grounding the film while retaining an unobtrusive stylistic flair. The manic camera during the dance scene, as Lulu and Déde plot how to rob Maurice, creates an intensity that runs contrary to the waltz-like ballad they slow dance to.

As a character study, the performances are the film's cornerstone. Here, Michel Simon's Maurice is the closest to a normal human being I've seen him (compared to his characters in <a href="https://boxd.it/e49hAV"><i>L'Atalante</i></a> and <a href="https://boxd.it/9reDXz"><i>Boudu Saved from Drowning</i></a>). Part of what sells the performance is Simon's actual crush on Janie Marèse, who plays Lulu. Also like the film, Marèse had a crush on Georges Flamant, who plays Déde.

The film had a tragic production. Flamant drove Marèse around for kicks in the car he bought with his wages from the film, Flamant's first. They got in a car wreck, and killed the 23-year-old actress. Simon blamed Renoir and was despondent at the funeral.

The story itself is little to write home about — such triangles were the topic of many a French novel and film. Renoir's style, his noir-like take on the material, and his focus on the everyman give this film a leg up. Perhaps nearly a century later, some of this style is invisible, but that only speaks to how well the film has aged.
