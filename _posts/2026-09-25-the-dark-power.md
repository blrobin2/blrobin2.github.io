<blockquote><i>Long before the white man came to America, even before the Aztecs, there existed the Toltecs. It was claimed that many were sorcerers who buried themselves in the ground — alive! They practiced this demonic ritual on high ground — "Power Spots." And they fed on the living to sustain their evil!!!!</i></blockquote>

<b>Southeastern United States</b>

In a house on Totem Hill, John "Four Eagles" Cody lay dying in bed, clinging to a statue of an eagle atop a skull. John is a beloved member of the community, known as a spiritual mystic, which also draws ire from others. A camera crew watches him from the corner of the room, capturing John's last will and testament on video. The man calls out the name Toltec before dying. The reporter plans to air parts of the last will and testament, for some reason.

John's grandson, David, arrives at the house on Totem Hill in what looks like a paddle boat. He meets Girard, claims the place as his own, and says he will not tolerate any more "hocus pocus." But Girard, a close friend of John's, reminds David that John left the house to some impoverished indigenous mystics who practice John's religion. They are to continue protecting the land from dark powers.

College student Beth didn't make the cut for local living opportunities. Lynn is also looking for a place, though she tries to act nonchalant. Beth's church friend suggests the two live together, exciting Lynn and bumming out Beth. Mary tells them about the Totem Hill house, and they decide it's big enough for a whole bunch of girls. Sort of like a sorority.

So, after 20 minutes, the film justifies why a Mexican tribe is buried in Kernersville, NC, and why there will be so many scenes of college-aged women wearing skimpy clothes.

The script feels like the writer kept writing ridiculous lines of dialogue, then making another character justify them. I've never seen a movie work so hard to justify every choice it makes, from Teltec's backstory to why there's a small fridge in a big house. Those choices made me laugh so much. The "comedic" beats, not so much.

If you couldn't tell from the first-act synopsis, this film is, uh, problematic. Characters rant about  "Indians" and "kicking red ass." Still, the film wants you to believe it's on the side of Indigenous people, with several scenes talking about how noble and self-sacrificing John was.

Beyond that, Lynn is so openly racist against Black people — she does NOT want Tammie living in the house, and that is the only reason she gives. Yes, she drops the n-word. Also, her brother, Craig, and his friends are CONSTANTLY abusing the women in the house — lifting skirts, groping, watching while bathing. Yes, they are the first few to die, but horror movies seem to think men deserve to live unless they sexually assault someone.

The monster effects are incredibly racist, but also pretty neat-looking. The gore, while cheap, is pretty messed up! I wasn't expecting it to go so hard! Like, "rip the skin off a dudes skull" hard. The sound mixing is ROUGH. It was so hard to hear several conversations while sound effects and score blare.

Still, this movie is surprisingly better-produced than Phil Smoot's other movie, <i><a href="https://boxd.it/bNbw3H" rel="nofollow">Alien Outlaw</a></i>, which came out the same year. Perhaps the horror formula is easier to emulate than sci-fi. I'm not saying it's good — it really isn't — but for low-budget regional horror, I've seen so much worse.
