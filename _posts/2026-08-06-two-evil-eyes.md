<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qa26g/detail" rel="nofollow">Cult Movie Challenge 2019 | 29/52 | Tom Savini</a></b>

The film started as an anthology, with Dario Argento initiating the project. Scheduling problems prevented John Carpenter, Clive Barker, or Wes Craven from joining in. Instead, we get two slightly longer segments from George Romero and Dario Argento.

As a result, we get two wildly different takes on Poe from two wildly different directors. What unites them, besides being Poe stories, is Tom Savini's grotesque special effects. It's all decently put together, even if none of it is all that surprising.

<b>The Facts in the Case of Mr. Valdemar (Romero)</b>

Jessica Valdemar visits her husband Ernest's lawyer, requesting that Mr. Pike liquidate Ernest's assets into cash. Pike checks the signature on the papers, calling them into question, so Jessica calls Ernest. Pike advises him against it, but Ernest insists it's okay. Ernest's doctor, Robert Hoffman, cuts in to assure him that Ernest is perfectly capable of making this decision.

Back home, she makes a drink with Robert. Ernest is still under hypnosis, fully aware that they are controlling him, making him speak words he would never say otherwise, but has no control. Robert wants to rub it in, make Ernest suffer, but Jessica doesn't want to torture her husband. That night, Ernest cries out in pain, viciously cursing Jessica for spending his money.

The following day, Jessica picks up the cash from the bank. Back home, she sends the cleaning crew home. Robert has Ernest sign more papers under hypnosis. Robert and Jessica step away to have sex, but Ernest's vitals spike and he dies, still under hypnosis. They decide to wait until the money comes through before announcing his death, keeping him in the basement freezer.

They think they have it all under control until Jessica hears moans coming from the freezer. It sounds like Ernest.

<blockquote><i>They're coming for you, Jessica.</i></blockquote>

I appreciate Romero's contemporary take on the lesser-known Poe story. He wanted to do Masque of the Red Death, but Argento wanted everything to be modern-day. Then, Roger Corman decided to do a remake of his classic 1964 version, so it was totally off the table.

If you're a Romero fan, you won't see any of his visual style here. From how controlling Argento supposedly was and the commercial failure of Monkey Shines, I can see why Romero would be more reserved.

That said, the story does contain some of Romero's themes around aging and lack of agency, as he explored in The Amusement Park. Ernest is trapped between death and life, begging for a way out but under the control of greed and inhumanity.

<b>The Black Cat (Argento)</b>

Rod Usher arrives at a grotesque murder involving sawing a woman in half with a pendulum. Rod activates the blade to get more intense photos. While developing the photos, a black cat arrives — a new adoption from his girlfriend, Annabel. The cat hisses and scratches Rod, establishing their hate-hate relationship.

Rod tries to get recognized for his photography, but all he has are crime scene photos. So, he sets up a photoshoot with the cat. In his rage, he strangles the cat to death. Annabel calls him out on it, and he attacks her. That night, Rod has a nightmare of townspeople bringing the hanged cat to Annabel, and they throw him on a spike.

The crime scenes start hitting Rod a little harder. Annabel has a younger side piece and makes plans to leave Rod after seeing his photobook featuring a strangled cat. But things get strange when Rod picks up a black cat from a local bar.

<blockquote><i>Cats remember, so they don't trust.</i></blockquote>

This segment is far more prurient and surreal (that is, leans closer to Argento's sensibilities), further reinforcing my belief that Argento bullied Romero into the short we got. That's not to say that this one is better, necessarily, although I think it will probably be to more gorehounds' tastes.

Harvey Keitel is so evil! I felt so bad for the kitty, Cinzentinha, although I could tell they didn't hurt her. I appreciate that the story gives our lead no redeemable qualities, but it sort of takes the moral out of Poe's story. In the original, the guy had some sense of remorse and guilt — Keitel just plays this guy like he's annoyed about the whole affair.

Argento's script puts endless Poe references in the character names, with Rod Usher, Annabel, Eleonora, the Pyms, and so on. And, of course, the opening murder weapon is the pendulum. By being based on a Poe story, this is perhaps Argento's most coherent story. The final reveal is sick.
