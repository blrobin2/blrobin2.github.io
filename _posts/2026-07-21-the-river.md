Harriet, an Englishwoman, grew up on the banks of the Ganges, a holy river in India whose waters come from the melting snow of the Himalayas. This story is about her first love, when she was a teenager — a relatively universal experience made specific by the confluence of her life and the lives of those around her. The fishermen who work the rivers, singing songs while they work out the knots in their net while their child sleeps. The jute farmers bringing in their crop from the flat marshes to the factories.

Harriet's father runs the jute press. After work, he comes home through the bazaar, making friends and buying gifts for his children. Harriet has four sisters and a brother, all much younger than her. Their wealth affords them the privilege of living their English lifestyle with little outside interference. Valerie, the daughter of the jute press owner, often comes by, even wealthier than Harriet's family. Besides Harriet's family, they have several live-in Indian employees, such as Nan, the nurse, who tells the children romantic stories.

One day, the weekly steamer brings a young American man, a cousin of their neighbor, the widower Mr. John, named Captain John. Straight away, Harriet finds Captain John beautiful. Valerie also seems keen on him, though she is less inclined to admit it. They all gather to write a letter, inviting him to their Diwali celebration. Valerie is closer in age to Captain John and draws his attention more quickly.

The film follows Harriet as she, Valerie, and Mr. John's daughter, Melanie, vie for Captain John's attention while navigating the shifting tides of life and growing up. But what they may not realize is why this former soldier is here in India at all.

<blockquote><i>The river runs, the round world spins. Dawn and lamplight, midnight, noon. Sun follows day, night, stars and moon. The day ends, the end begins.</i></blockquote>

The film provides the familiar frame up to that point of a white British audience surrogate taking us into the "unknown." But this film does not wish to engage in the orientalist fantasy that white filmgoers had come to expect.

Director Jean Renoir said he wanted to make "a film about India without elephants and tiger hunts." He was desperate to make something authentic, and worked with the story's author, Rumer Godden, to at the very least make it true to her understanding, having grown up in a life not too different from Harriet's. Harriet explains the customs and rituals as she understands them through narration, giving us a reverent meditation on them.

Most striking, however, is Melanie's involvement in the romance. Born to an Irish father and an Indian mother, she drew controversy from all audiences. True to his filmmaking credo, Renoir is content to depict, and neither condone nor condemn.

It's so cool that director Jean Renoir met with Satyajit Ray, who was then only a student. That's also when Ray met Subrata Mitra, who worked as a production assistant on this film and later became a cinematographer for several of Ray's pictures, including <i><a href="https://boxd.it/5kv2RD">Pather Panchali</a></i>, which came out four years later. Perhaps this is the greatest gift that Renoir gave us from this movie.

Also, this movie features one of the coolest trees, and its pristine cinematography captures the tree perfectly, especially near the end.
