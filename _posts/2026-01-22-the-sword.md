A blacksmith performs the finishing touches on the Hon Sing Sword, his final sword, which he intends to give to Fa Chin-shu. Fa Chin-shu brings the Qiwu Sword to the blacksmith with the intent of making it a matching pair to the Hon Sing. But the blacksmith advises Fa Chin-shu to throw it away, saying the sword was forged by men full of hatred and is therefore bad luck. The blacksmith believes it will bring harm to Fa Chin-shu, or even kill him.

Lee Mak-yin visits a man who sends and receives messages through carrier pigeons. He asks the man for the location of Fa Chin-shu. Lee Mak-yin pays the gold cost, and the man gives him a map to Fa Chin-shu's location. There, Lee Mak-yin meets a man who believes Lee is Fa Chin-shu, whom he has waited on for 12 years so that he may fight him. The man draws his sword and attacks, but Lee Mak-yin remains on the defensive. He holds the sword to the man's neck, and the man forces the sword to cut his own head off.

That night, while Lee Mak-yin sleeps, a woman named Fa Ying-chi enters the bedroom and hides under the sheets with him, holding her sword to his neck as she hides from a pursuer. Fa Ying-chi flies out the window, runs through the woods, and hides under a bridge, waiting to strike the man. Lee Mak-yin appears and fights the man off. The two decide to travel together as Lee Mak-yin continues his search for Fa Chin-shu. But that night, they stay at an inn where Lee Mak-yin's old flame, Yin Siu-yu, stays. Little does he know how much closer she will bring him to Fa Chin-shu.

<blockquote><i>Are all swordsmen lonely people?</i></blockquote>

By the late 70s, Golden Harvest was struggling to produce movies after Bruce Lee's death. Seasonal Film had picked up Jackie Chan and was making bank. So, they went to director Patrick Tam, who was working in TV at the time.

Tam sought to make a sort of revisionist wuxia, focusing on ambience over fights and encouraging the actors to develop unique choreography informed by their characters, rather than the usual approach of working out the choreo on the day of the shoot. Most importantly, Tam wanted to "deconstruct the hero image" by giving us a protagonist who remains passive throughout the film, and who must learn the physical and emotional cost of his journey.

The cinematography is solid, and the score is synth-driven and lush. The transitions are soft, with crossfades that feel as translucent as the fabric the women in the movie wear. The settings have a sense of decay, signaling the last remnants of a bygone era. The atmosphere is definitely there. Most of Lee Mak-yin's fighting is defensive — he opts to keep his sword sheathed unless necessary. He abandoned Yin Siu-yu for his journey, and it cost him more than he realized. He does not master his fate — he becomes a fool to it.

The film is a little tricky because it spends the first half setting up all the characters, which makes it feel almost episodic. But once we reach the mid-point, it all starts tying together quite nicely. The set pieces also pick up, so don't assume the movie's tone will stay in that dreamy space. There are some truly stunning segments throughout.

Patrick Tam was one of the seminal figures in the first Hong Kong New Wave, along with Tsui Hark, Ann Hui, and others. I think I've seen so many Tsui Hark movies that also incorporate some of the genre innovations that Patrick Tam did here, so it's hard to recognize just how essential a film this is to the genre. That said, I appreciated the movie's vibes.

Also, this is just a problem with the film still not having a release through a boutique company, but the lack of subtitles for written letters and other on-screen writing surely takes away from the whole story the film is telling.
