<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qa26g">Cult Movie Challenge 2019 | 20/52 | Haunted House</a></b>

<b>Los Angeles, 1967</b>

A sign reads "Madame Zander, Fortune Teller" outside an average house in the suburbs. Inside, we watch Madame Zander give a reading to Mr. Browning and his daughter, Jenny. Candles go out, the table shakes, and a moving shape nearly gives Mr. Browning a heart attack. But he leaves believing his wife is happy and full of love for him. After the show, Alice chastises her daughter Lina for scaring Mr. Browning and lets her younger daughter, Doris, out. Still, Lina suggests they spice up the act a bit.

Lina sneaks out to a party at Ellie's. Ellie suggests they play on her mom's Ouija board. Betty is reticent to join in and is scared when the planchette moves, but Paulina assures her that there is nothing on the other side and it's all just psychology. Ellie's mom walks in on the party and calls the parents. Alice comes to pick her up, Doris asleep in the backseat. After a talking-to, Paulina suggests Alice add a Ouija board to the act.

The next day, after the girls go to Catholic school, Alice picks up some candles and spots a Ouija board for sale. After picking up her daughters, she brings it home and modifies the board, introducing magnets and other ways to manipulate it without her hands. She tests the board out, breaking rule one (Don't play alone), but Doris, upstairs and out of earshot of the seance room, seems to answer Alice's questions as someone named Marcus. Once Alice closes the session, Doris returns to normal as though nothing had happened, freaking out Lina.

Soon, Doris starts to use the board to speak to her dad. And it works! But is it actually her dad, or is Marcus sticking around?

The film benefits from being a prequel, as the first movie firmly establishes the reality of Ouija, so with each interaction over the board, we wonder which ones are real and which aren't until it becomes quite clear. What I love about the setup is that it isn't about the Ouija board's reality. Rather, it's about who they are communicating with.

Part of why Doris is so susceptible is how dishonest Lina and Alice are with her. They don't think she can handle the reality of her father's death and make no attempt to help her. So, when Doris uses the same mechanisms as her mother for trying to speak to her dad, who is to blame but them?

I love almost all the ways they depict the spirits — background, blurry, out of focus — I found the CGI a little goofy. Not that it changes my score for the movie; it's just so ineffective compared to the rest of the movie.

The script suffers from some cliches during the emotional beats. Not a ton, but enough that I found myself pulled out when I heard lines I'd heard verbatim elsewhere, and from characters for whom they didn't necessarily fit. Also, the third act is <i>messy</i>.

As with a lot of Mike Flanagan's projects, the setup for this movie is empathetic and engaging. Still, by the time the end rolls around, it loses tension in the effort to elicit emotional beats that are too broad and saccharine.

Still, I enjoyed enough of it and can appreciate the movie's efforts, even if they don't all pay off.

#### Stray Thoughts / Spoilers

- The movie's wig budget was… not great. It's hard to get some of these period hairstyles with wigs.
- Betty is so me — when someone at a party wants to play any type of game, I'd also say, "We could just make conversation."
- It's funny that they clearly break two Ouija rules — don't play alone, and always say goodbye — but the one they use to justify the haunting is "playing in a graveyard." Maybe that's a reference to the first movie?
