The film moves through several genres, not unlike Lola, who seems to have no true identity throughout. Lola is not just the prototype of the manic pixie dream girl, but the culmination — it's all fun and wacky until the history that both her and Charley are hiding from catches up with them.

The film is also a scathing takedown of the New York underground. What may have been a thriving ecosystem at one point quickly became fodder for daytime TV and PSAs about what your children are listening to. Talking Heads and the like were making records built on stolen Afro-Caribbean music and aesthetics.

Lola, a fake from moment one, wears the Reggae-inspired colors when she first picks up Charley. Charley, a yuppie whose idea of rebellion is stealing food from a local restaurant in a low-income neighborhood, buys it hook, line, and sinker.

Blackness is a decoration to the movie, pushed to the periphery as if to acknowledge that the white people we're watching are all fakes, but without doing any of the work of bringing the Black people into it. The film is gentrification — it does more to prove the emptiness of the scene it portrays than to celebrate it.

What I like most about the movie is how it challenges Charley's fantasy, especially once Ray shows up. Ray is what Charley thinks he wants to be, so he ingratiates himself with him, only to learn that Ray is what the underbelly of Lola's vacuous life amounts to. Every time the movie seems poised for a Hollywood ending, it subverts it in some way (until the hack final scene).

Depending on how you read this movie, I could see one person loving it and another hating it. I appreciate it as an artifact and an indictment of the very culture that made it.
