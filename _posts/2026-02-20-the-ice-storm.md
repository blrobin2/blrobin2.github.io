<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qaTwm/detail">Asian Cinema Challenge 2023 | 49/52 | Directed by Ang Lee</a></b>

<blockquote><i>[…] a family is like your own personal antimatter. Your family is the void you emerge from and the place you return to when you die. And that's the paradox: the closer you're drawn back in, the deeper into the void you go.</i></blockquote>

Paul Hood reads the latest Fantastic Four comic while sitting on a stopped train. The lights flicker, and the train restarts after ice causes a malfunction. As daylight comes, the train arrives in New Canaan, Connecticut. At the train station, waiting are Paul's father, Ben, his mother, Elena, and his sister, Wendy.

Flash back to Paul in class, where they discuss Dostoyevsky's Notes from the Underground. Paul's crush, Libbets, speaks up in class, so after class, Paul tries to impress her by bringing up The Idiot. Paul made a mistake by telling his roommate, Francis, about his crush, and Francis notoriously hooks up with everyone Paul shows an interest in. Ben calls Paul to make sure he's coming up for Thanksgiving while Wendy watches Richard Nixon give his "I am not a crook" speech post-Watergate. Ben forces Wendy to say hi to Paul while he and Elena get ready to visit the Carver family.

At the dinner, the Hoods join Ted and Dorothy Franklin in a dinner party with Jim and Janey Carver, while the Carver sons, Mikey and Sandy, serve them. Mikey accidentally pours some wine on Ben's lap, and Janey is awfully casual in helping Ben wipe down the front of his pants. Elena tries to help Janey clean up, but Janey angrily sends Elena away. The next day, Elena runs into the Reverend from a church she checked out a while back. He offers therapy for Elena or her family. She asks him about bike riding.

The film follows the Hoods and Carvers as their lives implode in that classic suburbanite way.

The dialogue is heavy-handed, with none of it sounding like a human being. They're blunt, eloquent, incisive in ways that feel rehearsed and polished. That's likely Rick Moody's fault, whose book serves as the basis for this film. It's not unbearable, but with some characters, such as Francis, it pulled me out of the movie.

Also, the 70s decor and clothing are exaggerated, highlighting the decade's tackiest aspects. Like, does Sigourney Weaver need to be reading When She Was Good in a corduroy dress pants while lying on a waterbed?

That said, the film is more somber and reflective than its surfaces suggest. It spends time with its characters, especially its female characters, giving us glimpses into their being but no concrete explanation for why, beyond the obvious sadness that runs throughout everyone. The film also understands the horrors and foibles of puberty.

Thematically, the film explores the contrast between adults reminiscing about their misspent youth and young adults dead set on doing what they think adults do. In every case, life doesn't pan out the way the characters expect. Sometimes, the fantasy is best left a fantasy.

I know I complained about the dialogue, but the script does have some funny bits that left me chuckling throughout. It's hard for me not to see the movie as the proto-<i>American Beauty</i>. But, as of 2026, this film is far less melodramatic and more insightful, even if it didn't hit me the way it has others.
