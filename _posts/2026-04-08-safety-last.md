The Boy, Harold Lloyd, stands behind bars. His mother and girlfriend, Mildred, hold his hands and console him. A uniformed man tells him it's time. In the background swings what appears to be a noose. The camera pivots, and it becomes clear that Harold is at the Great Bend Train Station — the noose, a mail book installed along the railroad track. The Boy is off to the big city to start a new life. He'll send for Mildred once he's established himself so that they can get married.

A few months pass. The Boy rents a room with Limpy Bill. The two don't have much between them and are perpetually late on rent. The Boy comes home to show Limpy Bill a lavalier he got for Mildred, but he hasn't been able to afford a chain for it yet. And how did The Boy afford the lavalier? He pawned their record player. The Boy writes to Mildred daily, desperate to convince her that he's doing well. He sends her the lavalier, along with a letter fibbing about his successes.

The Boy makes what little he can as a salesclerk at the De Vore Department Store. Despite showing up early and working hard, his luck always seems to draw him into trouble. Mr. Stubbs, the head floorwalker, has it in for The Boy, so The Boy must pull outrageous stunts to avoid his ire. Things escalate when The Boy's mother encourages Mildred to go to the city to see her successful fiancé.

Hijinks ensure!

<blockquote><i>Young man, don't you know you might fall and get hurt?</i></blockquote>

Anyone who has followed me for a bit knows I love <a href="https://boxd.it/5jxtkV">Harold</a> <a href="https://boxd.it/91yAW5">Lloyd</a>. His physical stunts are always impressive and serve the story. The most exciting part of the film is the second half, which centers on climbing a 12-story building as a promotional stunt for the department store. Each floor has its own set of troubles that Lloyd must navigate.

Though we know stunt doubles were used in some places and that the buildings were never as high as the camera made them appear, the physical comedy Lloyd uses to sustain this extended set piece holds it all together quite beautifully. What makes this movie stand the test of time is the craftsmanship put into the filmmaking — unless you know the behind-the-scenes, you will believe that Lloyd is in terrific danger.

The film gives a portrait of working-class life in the 20s, whether it means to or not — meager pay that barely covers the rent, arbitrary hierarchies where customer service is a hellish torment, and being cruel means you've made it, and desperate stunts in hopes of that life-saving cash-out. The high-rise climb is surely a metaphor for the corporate ladder that few succeed in climbing, and most wouldn't dare. By the 20s, I mean, of course, the 2020s.

While the film never achieves the death-defying stunt work of Buster Keaton, it still offers plenty of severe injury-defying feats. The laughs mostly come from the high-flying tension, but there are plenty of small gags that drew chuckles from me.
