Cabiria and Giorgio run through the field and to a river. Giorgio looks around, then pushes Cabiria into the water, taking her purse and running off while she drowns. A young boy hears Cabiria yelling and calls for help. The men retrieve her and perform some pre-heimlich maneuver to get the water out of her. Several declare her dead, but she coughs up water and comes to.

Cabiria races back to her humble house, looking for Giorgio but not finding him. Without her purse, Cabiria has to enter her place through the windows. Her neighbor, Wanda, stays up late to console her, but Cabiria tires of her and tells her off. Cabiria throws Giorgio's stuff into a fire kids have outside, cursing him but brokenhearted.

Later that night, Wanda hangs out with other sex workers, like herself, and their pimps. Cabiria rides up on the back of a motorbike. One sex worker makes a joke about Giorgio, and she and Cabiria get into a fight. The other women put her in the back of Amleto's car, and they, along with Marisa, ride away. They dump the enraged Cabiria out on the street.

The film follows Cabiria as she tries to discover what needs to change for her to have a better life. During Cabiria's nights, she finds fleeting moments of happiness among the complacency of wealth, the mystery of charity, the mercy of the divine, and the love of the idea of a person.

The film continues Fellini's transition from neorealism to metaphorical and autobiographical work. This film has neorealist qualities, focusing on the lives of sex workers and their hardships. But with Giulietta Masina's performance, the movie takes on a comedic quality. Masina has something of a Lucille Ball-like intensity. Her performance is what shifts the message from "Life is misery" to "Life's so miserable that you gotta laugh."

The lighting and cinematography are so tight that it's easy to take them for granted. The streets at night look gorgeous, as though they might contain the promise Cabiria is looking for. Even when Cabiria is at her lowest, the world glows for her. The music cues get some laughs too, such as Lazarri's closet jingle and the triumphant music when Lazarri orders room service. It also softens some of the misery with tender interludes and sweeping movements that end on major keys.

A recurring motif is people parading through the streets, whether for religious observance or young people heading out for the night. In each sense, they represent a sort of hope — that, even when it hurts the most, life goes on.

I am so deeply charmed and moved by this movie! Giulietta Masina gives one of my all-time favorite performances. Also, one of my favorite final shots in any movie.
