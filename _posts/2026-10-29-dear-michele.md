Adriana Vivanti and her ex-husband have the same ritual every Thursday. They meet at the same restaurant table and sit from 5 to 7 PM. They hate it and don't talk, but the ritual is familiar. This Thursday, however, he doesn't show. She rushes to his house to discover he is having surgery. He is also renovating a seaside tower he purchased for their son, Michele.

Adriana calls Michele to come over, but no one answers. So she calls Osvaldo, a friend of Michele's who lives nearby. Osvaldo tells her he took Michele to the airport, where he flew to London, where he intends to spend the winter. We soon learn that Michele is a revolutionary, and after his friend Anselmo is arrested, he goes into hiding.

Adriana also receives a letter from Mara Castorelli, whom she doesn't recall meeting, but who wants to show her the baby. Little does she know the baby may be Michele's. Osvaldo tries to help Mara get a housekeeping job for the Peronis, but Mara's self-centered and naive approach stresses them out. So, they kick her out, and she moves into Michele's empty flat.

Michele has his sister, Angelica, clean up his life in Rome. She throws a dismantled machine gun he was hiding into the river. She hosts his London friend, Ray, at her husband Oreste's expense. Amidst all of this, Angelica's father dies. Mara's life is falling apart. Will the Vivantis be the glue to bring it back together?

<blockquote><i>— Why do you speak of Michele as if he were dead?<br>
— Because I think we'll never see him again.</i></blockquote>

The title refers to the many letters written to Michele throughout the movie. We hear his voice once in a letter he writes, but never see his face. These letters catch us up on the minutiae of life around the movie, keeping us invested in their lives. They also let us into the interior worlds of characters who might otherwise seem flat.

Mara is the comedic center of an otherwise fairly grounded family drama. She steals her neighbors' stuff, wakes the Peronis at all hours to feed and change her baby, and accidentally floods their place when she turns on the tap and locks herself out. As the film progresses, however, we see how much class shapes their differences. Mara doesn't fit in with the Vivanti family's performative intelligentsia.

Clearly, there are some political undertones, with Michele being a revolutionary. The film seems to be dealing with a post-1968 world. The Vivantis' bourgeois lifestyle doesn't seem to shield them from the pain of unhappy marriages and settling down. Oreste beats Ray's face in for being a "reactionary," forcing him to move out. Viola, Angelica's sister, is convinced Michele is gay, or at least bisexual. Ray uses Michele's basement to host all sorts of beatnik-adjacent folks.

This movie has such a stacked crew! Nino Rota's score is sentimental, grounding the movie's silliness with an emotional core. Tonino Delli Colli's cinematography adapts to whatever the scene needs. It's wild that he shot this one year after Salò. Italian Neorealism founder Suso Cecchi d'Amico and regular Antonioni collaborator Tonino Guerra write the script, but I know too little about their work to pinpoint anything.

The film is full of tiny details that bring it to life. Michele's place is decorated with unfinished paintings. His father was also a painter. Adrianna hires Cloti, a lazy, mean-spirited maid, and moves her sister-in-law, Matilde, in with her. Adriana moved her and the kids out to the country because Filippo got married and is expecting a child. Who is Filippo? You find out an hour in that he is her former lover.

That said, this surplus of details may also be to the film's detriment, as it is difficult to juggle all the threads. It's clear the screenwriters treated Natalia Ginzburg's novel with some care, but they struggle to balance the Vincenti family plotline with Mara's antics.

I enjoyed large parts of this movie. I don't know if I love it as a whole, but I became so invested in the characters' lives. I'm glad I have access to this seemingly rare film, and I could see myself revisiting it now that I know the whole thing and feel differently.
