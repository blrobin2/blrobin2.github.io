Two months ago, Detective Fisher left his wife in Cairo while he chased a police job in Europe. Since then, he has had headaches and a lingering obsession with Europe, though he cannot remember much of what happened to him. So, he undergoes hypnosis to relive the memories of Europe, his first time there in over 13 years.

In his memory, everything is underwater — a sink, barbed wire, a dead horse. Then he is aboard a boat at night, with a spotlight illuminating the water. He tries to dock at Osborne's house, but the stairs have moved. The housekeeper guides Fisher inside and to Osborne, drowning himself in drink. Fisher gives Osborne a copy of his book, The Element of Crime, translated into Egyptian. But Osborne has sold all his books, thrown away all his work, and disowned his framework, which focused on society, in favor of searching for the nature of man.

The phone rings. Osborne hides. Fisher answers. It's Chief Inspector Kramer, who gave Fisher the job. They've found another mutilated girl by the harbor. Fisher drives a bullet-pocked police car to the docks, draped in a fishing net and littered with loose newspapers. Children point to the coal stack, from which men lower the girl's body. A man driving a horse cart full of apples falls into the water. A man with a rifle aims it at the horse and kills it. Kramer has left the city in chaos.

Lottery tickets spray across the dirt. Fisher finds a horse talisman and pockets it. A young girl runs by, and Kramer shoots at her. Fisher stops him and questions the girl — the one who died was her sister. They both sell lottery tickets. The ones who are dying are the ticket sellers. So, Fisher starts the job the way Osborne trained him to — he attempts to simulate the life of the murderer so he can get into his mind.

The film is rife with stylistic flourishes. I'm not opposed to them, but it does reek of a first-time director pouring everything from his head into the movie. The layers of abstraction don't always serve the story, but it's clear that von Trier is trying to push into more experimental territory.

From the onset, Lars von Trier has made his fixation on Tarkovsky known. The opening scene of the donkey struggling to stand is reminiscent of a shot from <i><a href="https://boxd.it/3EAtcB">Andrei Rublev</a></i>. Fisher's first memories of Europe are all underwater — the camera scans over the water like moments from <i><a href="https://boxd.it/2iK5pb">Stalker</a></i>. Also, the film is shot with sodium light to give it a similar sepia tone to the monochrome scenes in <i>Stalker</i>. There are plenty of others throughout, as well as references to other filmmakers, I'm sure.

The film also has that sort of noir feel — angular light, sharp compositions, and dour reflection staining the whole experience. But the world has dissolved into the dissociations of memory, where fragments poke through one another, insinuating juxtaposition and heightening the mystery to an almost unknowable degree. It gives the movie a sort of surrealism I would associate with Terry Gilliam, but more absurdist.

The film uses these abstractions to represent a society in collapse. The old ways haunt the spaces — the conversations around methods and ideas reflect how one generation's ideals are the next generation's problems to solve. Violence erupts at the edges like a post-apocalyptic wasteland. Disorder hides simple truths, just like any mystery. This is not the world as it is, but the world as Fisher recalls it through his hypnotic recollection. But it isn't an inaccurate representation.

Perhaps the viewer may feel that the indirection is not artistic intent as much as an artist trying to hide something in plain sight. But this film's issues are a far cry from the masturbatory, self-referential work von Trier has put out more recently. Still, the film's meandering middle, following Harry Grey, is hard for any viewer — even the film has a metanote from the hypnotist, saying, "It doesn't seem connected to the story."

This movie is quite a wild debut! It all comes together in its own way.
