<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail" rel="nofollow">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 41/52 | Film Noir</a></b>

<blockquote><i>So gather around as I run it down and unravel my pedigree.</i></blockquote>

<b>Cleveland, 1972</b>

It's Christmastime and snow is falling. Russell Stevens sits in the car with his father as his father snorts a bump. His dad asks him what he wants for Christmas as he loads a handgun, stepping out to hold up a liquor store. A gunshot, and his dad is outside with cash, brandishing it to his son. The liquor store owner, still barely alive, shoots his dad with a shotgun, killing him. Stevens grew up with the image of himself holding his father's dead body, and the singular thought that it wasn't going to happen to him.

<b>Twenty Years Later</b>

DEA Agent Gerry Carver interviews several black police officers individually, asking them the difference between a black man and an n-word. The last officer interviewed is Stevens, who is the only one who can answer the chief's question. So Carver offers Stevens a deep-cover job in LA, where he would pose as a drug dealer. He pulls out Stevens' psych profile, on which Stevens scores "almost exactly like a criminal."

<blockquote><i>Undercover, all your faults will become virtues.</i></blockquote>

<b>Los Angeles, Two Weeks Later</b>

Stevens knew that taking this job would be the worst decision of his life. But took it he did. He meets his new neighbors, Belinda Chacon and her young son. Carver gives him the rundown of the heavy hitters: Felix Barbosa, Barbosa's boss Anton Gallegos, and Gallegos' uncle Hector Guzmán, a Latin-American politician. Going after them in that order will help him work his way up the network.

Stevens becomes John Hull. Things start well until David Jason, a supplier's supplier and an attorney, quickly recognizes that John is a cop.

Originally based on a nonfiction account of a DEA operation gone awry, the script (written by two white men) initially had a white lead. But New Line Cinema saw the success of films like <i>Boyz n the Hood</i> and saw an opportunity to capitalize on a "niche market." What helps this movie accomplish more is Bill Duke's direction. Duke is an actor's director who likes letting his actors improvise, which gives the film so much of its color and the characters so much, well, weirdness.

Despite the intensity of its subject matter, the film isn't chasing realism. Instead, the film looks through the lens of Stevens, not naive in his experience but in his interpretation of it. He saw the pain and misery of the streets and thought that being a cop was the way to fix it. Everyone that Stevens doesn't respect gives cartoonish performances, but filtered through Stevens' lens, they make sense.

Laurence Fishburne balances anger with an earnest desire to suppress it at all costs. The world sees him one way, and he's dead set to prove them wrong.  His narration hits the PERFECT "film noir detective looking back on his life with regret" tone. He forms a bond with Belinda's son, James, seeing in Belinda a lot of what he saw in his father. He falls for Betty, who helps launder drug money.

Detective Taft is, in some ways, the person Stevens thinks he is: the good Christian who wants to take the gun away from the heads of his children — the man who actually believes that the War on Drugs is a Good Thing. Stevens wants to believe it, but learns over and over throughout how much of a game it all is. To be black in America is to be two people at all times, forced to see yourself through the eyes of whiteness.

<blockquote><i>You and me are one. Don't forget who you are.</i></blockquote>

The script, despite its oversimplification of the drug world, has the full sociopolitical scope, seeing how criminals in high enough places are useful to the US government. It never hides how much race plays into the war on drugs and the way whiteness preys on minorities to keep the cycle going. The cinematography is probably the film's weakest facet, with conventional shots that drain several scenes of the tension in the script and performances. Still, the performances make up for it. The score fucking rules. From the first moment, I was in a trance.

Unlike nearly every drug movie I've seen, this movie isn't copaganda. It's messy. And that's why this movie, despite its familiar trappings, transcends whatever genre you assign to it: it's not film noir or a "hood film." If anything, it's a fantasy. Like all good fantasy, it uses the aesthetics of reality to say something realism cannot access.

<blockquote><i>So what exactly have you accomplished with this vast expenditure of time, money, and violence?</i></blockquote>

At one point, Jeff Goldblum is dressed exactly like Neo at the end of <i>The Matrix</i>, and I couldn't help but imagine a world where Jeff Goldblum was Neo.
