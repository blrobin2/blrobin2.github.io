When I first watched this movie, I mostly wrote it off as yet another found-footage film. And it is definitely that. But this time around, I was more ready to appreciate the feminist themes throughout.

Aaron has not learned the social cues and red flags that Josef throws at him from the moment they meet—social cues that women must learn to survive men. Josef uses the abuse playbook: forced touch, immediate and unwarranted affection, gaslighting, and so on. He weaponizes therapy-speak to put Aaron at ease, then pushes him deeper, numbing his sense of danger. He puts Aaron in the shoes that women traditionally fulfill in horror movies.

When Aaron takes the filming job, he believes he has some control over the situation. Aaron is played by Patrick Brice, the director of the movie, adding a meta-layer to the notion of control. In other words, the director is the victim in his film. This inversion speaks to the power dynamics in movies like <i>The Exorcist</i> or <i>The Shining</i>, where directors exerted control over the actresses, inflicting real-life harm to convey fictional horror.

The movie still has plenty of flaws and an easy-to-pick-apart plot. But the experience of watching the film, especially with someone who hadn't seen it before, is a treat.
