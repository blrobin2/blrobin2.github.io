<blockquote><i>We all come from the same tree. No one dies. The father feeds the son. The ancestors feed the father. The heritage must not be dispersed.</i></blockquote>

After being sent away many years ago, Dou returns to the village of Amanha Lundju. In the middle of the village lay the dead body of his twin, Hami. Dou approaches the two trees that his father planted when he and Hami were born and asks which one is dead. A villager called The Mute leads Dou to where the villagers are putting out a fire that threatens to overtake the village.

The people call for the village sorcerer, Calacalado, to come out of his hut and help them put out the fire. Reluctantly, he points his staff at the fires, and they retreat. Some men blame Eusebio for accidentally starting the fire, but Saly, Hami's widow, knows Hami's spirit is punishing her for losing a pendant. She finds the pendant, and the wind sings to her.

Dou must deal with people mistaking him for Hami: Saly, his mother, Puntcha, and Hami's son, N'te. Partly because Dou's tree, not Hami's, died. Before the fire, the birds had left the village because of Hami's death. With Dou's return, the birds return too. Calacalado tells Dou about Hami's anger, which consumed him. He must consult his father's tree to know what to do.

The film follows Dou and the villagers as they try to make sense of what has happened to Hami and what is happening to their trees.

<blockquote><i>What is our curse? The forest is burning. Some say it's [Hami]. Trees are being cut down. It's [Hami] again. For [his] soul to rest in peace, even trees can forgive.</i></blockquote>

The people of the village see themselves as two entities: one body and one tree. That is, the trees are human beings too. They live, they bleed, and they die. In tradition, when a mother gives birth to twins, only one twin is allowed to live, since their natural twin is the tree. Instead, Calacalado lets both live, sends Dou away, and believes he is facing the consequences.

This is a world where people and nature communicate freely together. The wind sings, the fires obey, and the ancestors send signs through natural occurrences. An adorable scene shows the village lifting all the babies to celebrate them before their baptism. They will also light candles and plant trees for them. That is, until the sound of chainsaws breaks the festival, and the disruption becomes clear.

The cinematography adds a poetic quality to the storytelling. For example, when Calacalado puts out the fire, the camera moves through the crowd, everyone looking at it, as if the camera were the magic itself that would put out the fire. The camera work is fluid as it gently moves through scenes.

The film also captures symbolic moments, such as Saly letting a bowl of milk overflow, watching the milk snake and weave across the dirt, into the trees. Dou's mother has a vision where she sees the women of her family caked in mud and the sheets stained a copper red, somewhere between blood and dirt. The finale uses red yarn to represent the destruction of their village and life, like guts strewn across a battlefield.

The film looks at human greed through the eyes of this village—humanity sees its trees not as living beings but as resources to be extracted and exploited. The greed that destroys the trees destroys humanity. Those who connect with nature more directly, who see themselves as part of it, have seen the cost that many are only now recognizing.
