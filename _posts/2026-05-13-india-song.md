<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E" rel="nofollow">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 15/52 | 1970s</a></b>

<blockquote><i>Dead on the islands. Her eyes pierced with light, dead. Beneath that stone. Around her, a bend in the Ganges.</i></blockquote>

The film opens on the setting sun, with unknown narrators singing/telling the story of a disturbed beggar woman who walks for ten years from Savannakhét, Laos, through Burma to the Ganges in Bengal, abandoning and selling her twelve children along the way. Her mother threw her out at 17, pregnant. In Calcutta, she met a white woman. A man followed the white woman to India, leaving everything behind. His name was Michael Richardson. He was engaged to a girl from S. Thala, but on the night of the ball, he met Anne-Marie Stretter, dressed in black. When Anne-Marie died out on the islands, Michael left, burying her in the English cemetery.

Now, it is monsoon season, and it is impossibly hot in Central Calcutta. Leprosy ravages the area, but we are in the safe confines of the French embassy in India, the babbling of the Ganges heard nearby. A French diplomat, Anne-Marie's husband, watches her dance with Michael. They hear a cry outside. The French vice-consul from Lahore stands at the shore, arriving in shame in Calcutta. At 4 AM, Anne-Marie lay on the floor in her black robe. The vice-consul from Lahore watches her from the doorway. Her husband watches from another. He joins her on the floor. Michael enters from a hidden passageway and lies beside Anne-Marie's husband.

Growing up in Venice, Anne-Marie was a promising pianist. She never gave up playing, but at 18 she married a French colonial civil servant and moved to Savannakhét. She spent her days looking into the Mekong River, unable to bear her life in guarded safety. Her second and current marriage is to the ambassador, Stretter, who moves her to several capitals before settling in Calcutta. The vice-consul from Lahore cries, falling in love with Anne-Marie, but never speaking to her.

The film follows Anne-Marie, the marital affairs her husband tolerates, and her slow death from "leprosy of the heart."

<blockquote><i>I'm listening to "India Song." I came to India because of "India Song." That tune makes me want to love. I have never loved. I had never loved anyone.</i></blockquote>

The film tells its story through an enigmatic choir of voices, asking poetic questions to arrive at reality. From their perspective, the whole story is in the past, but they do not tell the story in chronological order. They also seem to possess knowledge of incidental details that we must intuit from the ellipses and suggestions in their questions. In scenes with characters, they do not speak to one another — instead, the voices seem to speak for them, as though transmitting thoughts or interpreting the images. The camera slowly pans, watching scenes and landscapes with utmost patience.

This form of storytelling is quite singular, but it feels inspired by Jacques Lacan and his take on phenomenology, as he and Duras were friends in life. For those who don't know, phenomenology focuses on capturing the subjective through phenomena and lived experience, all to better understand the nature of consciousness. It contrasts with behaviorism, which reduces our lived experience to pure stimulus and response. By divorcing the stream-of-consciousness from the characters' observed behaviors, we experience a deeper meaning in their innocuous stares or movements that their words or thoughts could not convey.

Through the ennui of this wealthy woman, we see the decaying effects of colonialism on the body and soul. To occupy another's land as her own is to become a ghost, haunting a world that has nothing for her. Historically, men have taken women as prizes and conquests, like the lands they steal and settle in. These men starve their women in glass cages as though they were toys to fondle at their leisure. Anne-Marie is a prize, divorced from her home, her skills, and her happiness to serve the whims of the men who cross her path.

Through the vice-consul from Lahore, we also explore the dehumanization of the Indian people, specifically those with leprosy. Like the police in the US or rapist priests in the Catholic church, the vice-consul does not face any criminal charges for his actions, but is merely transferred somewhere else to become someone else's problem. In the background chatter of the choir, we also hear conversations regarding the culpability of the Western world and the denial that the white settlers hide beneath. The introduction of the "disturbed beggar woman" at the beginning of the movie only pays off later, when we learn why the choir labels her "disturbed." She, like many beggars, exists on the periphery of the embassy, but is never visible.

Ever since I saw <i>Hiroshima, Mon Amour</i> in college, which Marguerite Duras wrote, I've wanted to see her best-known directorial effort. I'm glad I waited until now, because my half-developed college brain would've glazed over while trying to understand what was at work here. What's fascinating is that I find this structurally closer to another movie Resnais directed that Duras did not write, <i>Last Year at Marienbad</i>. It's also fascinating that the following year, Duras put out a sequel, <i>Her Venetian Name in Deserted Calcutta</i>, that uses the same soundtrack of voices as this movie, only changing the images.

I understand why this movie is so divisive — you're either on board for the experiment or unbearably bored by the lack of conventional storytelling. The whole experience feels purely cinematic in that no other medium could capture this specific combination of images and words. If you do not pay attention to the choir, you may mistake this film for shallow or decadent — but, like all of white settler "culture," the atrocities and truth lie just beneath the facade, between the margins, rendered invisible by force.
