<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail" title="Criterion Challenge 2021 | 45/52 | Directed by Ingmar Bergman">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 45/52 | Directed by Ingmar Bergman</a>

Viktor, a pastor, tells the audience about his wife, Eva: how they met and how she felt at home in the parsonage. Before meeting him, she was an author of two books and a journalist. He doesn't know how to express his love for her, since beautiful words don't sway Eva. So, he finds himself watching her without her knowing.

<blockquote><i>One must learn to live. I practice every day. I grope blindly.</i></blockquote>

Eva brings Viktor a letter she wrote to her mother, Charlotte, inviting her to stay with them for a while after Charlotte lost her husband, Leonardo. They haven't seen one another in seven years. Charlotte, a renowned pianist, arrives and reminisces about the 18 years she spent with Leonardo and those final two years of illness. She tries to remain cheery.

Eva reveals that Helena is also joining them, having lived with Eva and Viktor for the past two years, which frustrates and deflates Charlotte. Helena was in hospital, but Eva preferred to have her at home. Charlotte begrudgingly visits Helena, who is paralyzed and has trouble communicating, so Eva has to help translate.

Charlotte's guilty conscience dogs her. She considers leaving early for a trip to Africa. Instead, she works with her manager, Paul, to schedule a performance. The film follows Eva and Charlotte as a lifetime of grievances and seven years of absence surface.

<blockquote><i>— I used to adore you. Then I got tired of you and your piano. Now, I adore you, but in a different way.<br>
— Then there's some hope.</i></blockquote>

This film was Ingmar and Ingrid Bergman's only collaboration. Ingrid's performance stands out, carrying years of anger and assumptions about people's worst tendencies. She thinks in terms of spite and retribution, even when people are kind to her. Still, she feels she must hide her pain.

Liv Ullmann's Eva is too raw and unforgiving of herself to convey anything with subtlety. She chooses simplicity and subservience, living as a wife and parish caretaker. Eva lost her son, Erik, who drowned before his fourth birthday. She believes that he is still alive in another world, comforting her.

The film uses Chopin's Prelude No. 2 in Am, through Eva and Charlotte's interpretations, to convey their internal worlds. Eva plays competently, and her performance moves her mother, but Eva awaits her mother's approach and the encroaching sense of inferiority.

Bergman's later films make room for characters to tell stories and reminisce. They speak aloud their internal monologues like Shakespearean asides, sometimes facing the camera. Though Ingmar Bergman went on to direct acclaimed television productions that were cut for theatrical release and made-for-TV movies, this was the last film that he made for theatrical release.

Perhaps Bergman's depression could not allow for any hope, but only the perpetuation of pain. Or perhaps he saw that the only way to stop the cycle was never to have kids at all. Regardless, this one doesn't offer any solace when the dark and lonely night creeps in.

<blockquote><i>— Eva, you do like me, don't you?<br>
— You're my mother.</i></blockquote>
