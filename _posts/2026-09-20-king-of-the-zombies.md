<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qa26g/detail" rel="nofollow">Cult Movie Challenge 2019 | 41/52 | Zombies</a></b>

Bill, Mac, and Jeff fly in a plane lost in a storm. Low on gas, they hear a radio transmission and go down to check it out. They wake up on an island and find an old, dark mansion. Knocking to no answer, they enter and find Dr. Miklos Sangre greeting them by candlelight.

Dr. Sangre warns them of evil spirits on the island. They ask about the radio, but the doctor assures them no radio exists for miles. He shows the white men rooms and sends Jeff to the servant quarters. There, he speaks to the other servants, who warn him of zombies that walk the house. Two come around and send Jeff to warn the others.

Dr. Sangre takes Bill, Mac, and Jeff down to the kitchen to follow up on Jeff's warning. The other servants claim that Jeff is making it all up. When the rest leave, the servants reveal that Dr. Sangre is the one making them. Bill and Mac meet Mrs. Sangre, who walks with a distant stare.

What's going on? Obviously Dr. Sangre is making zombies, but to what end?

The film plays on the classic "old dark house" trope, which I'm always a sucker for. It reminds me of Scooby-Doo. We get plenty of folks wandering around by candlelight and things not being what they seem.

The film also plays on classic spy tropes, with Bill being a US spy on a secret mission. He is also the most intellectually uncurious person, dismissing every clue that Mac and Jeff bring him. In other words, he's every white male lead in these movies.

Dr. Sangre and his family are ostensibly Austrian refugees. By this point, Hitler had annexed Austria into Nazi Germany, so perhaps that is what they were fleeing? It's curious, then, that he performs experiments that Nazis would, although I don't know how well-known this was at the time.

The film has several black actors of note who give the film much of its charm:

- Mantan Moreland, who plays Jeff, is the racist comedic relief as a superstitious black man. He's also the only voice of reason for large swaths of the movie. He speaks up for himself from time to time, even if the white characters routinely dismiss him.
- Marguerite Whitten often did films alongside Mantan Moreland, although she was rarely credited in her film roles.
- Madame Sul-Te-Wan, the first black actress to get a contract as a studio performer, makes an appearance as the voodoo priestess, Tahama.
- Leigh Whipper, who plays Momba the butler, was one of the founders of the Negro Actors Guild of America.
- Laurence Criner, a popular black actor who had many lead roles as early as the 20s, makes a brief appearance as Dr. Coullie.

The version on Tubi has some of the laziest, most haphazard colorization I've seen — it was quite distracting. The subtitles are atrocious, especially for the black characters.

Overall, while problematic and not totally cohesive, the film has its fair share of fun, funny moments that help it stand out from the standard fare of the time.
