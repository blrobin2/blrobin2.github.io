The sky glows an unnatural red. Shareen goes scavenging for a TV in the Fresh Kills Landfill on Staten Island. The news reports on the pollution affecting the city, causing the sky's red glow.

A sushi chef buys a special fish, Yamakazu, for its lips. One has a green glow, contaminated. At the sushi restaurant, Jiannbin preps a special order of Yamakazu for Honey.

Shareen picks up her partner, Claire. Back home, they watch TV with their daughter, Honey, whose mouth burns from all the extra wasabi Uncle Jiannbin put in the sushi.  On TV, they discuss a radiation leak from an American hydrogen bomb off the coast of Okinawa.

Mimi and Nina host a current events discussion show where they also advertise sex toys and condoms. Shareen goes down on Claire while she plays the accordion (or squeeze box, if you will). Jiannbin is a hacker. Miguel is a poet, always breaking the fourth wall. GX Corporation is running an ad claiming it cares.

Honey's hands start glowing a green the same as the fish. What's the connection between the poisoned fish and GX?

The film is unsubtle with its critique of American military intervention overseas, especially regarding the pollution they've left in the developing world from leaking nuclear armaments to oil spills into essential food biomes. Today, the American military is the number-one polluter and contributor to global warming. Television acts as the propaganda propagator, passing corporate and government cover-ups just under our noses.

The film intentionally messes with race. Shareen, who has Bengali heritage, has a father, Clayton, who is indigenous American—Claire, who is white, has a black daughter, Honey, with Shareen. White characters are openly racist, such as the businesswoman with an "Oriental thing" for Jiannbin.

The film takes on surreal, metaphorical dialogue and structure. It doesn't devolve out of the story, but it moves in fairly singular ways. The representation of hacking is not quite as silly as <i>Hackers</i>, but still leans graphical just to make it more interesting to look at. This film was the first to use the word "hacktivism," stemming from director Shu Lea Cheang's fascination with internet and hacker culture.

Vernon Reid, the founder of the band Living Colour, composed the movie's score. The music has an intense cheesiness, intentionally pushing into grating territory. It reminds me of Badalamenti's work with Lynch, but with more variety and personality.

The experimental quality of this movie makes it difficult to score on my usual scale. Still, I appreciated a lot about it, even if it didn't have an emotional impact on me.
