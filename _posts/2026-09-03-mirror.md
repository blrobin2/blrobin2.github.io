<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail" rel="nofollow">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 35/52 | Made in the Soviet Union</a></b>

A college-aged man turns on a TV, watching an interview with himself stuttering to introduce himself as Yuri. He works with a mentalist who helps him focus his tensions from his mind into his hands. Afterward, he can speak clearly.

Maroussia sits on a fence outside a homestead, watching a man slowly approach. He asks for directions, then a tool to open his doctor's bag. She is apprehensive to answer his questions. He sits on the fence with her, and the fence breaks. He waxes poetic about the sentience of plants and how we ignore our nature and rush around. As he leaves, a strong wind picks up, saying goodbye.

Outside, a couple yells and a dog barks — there's a fire. We transition to the couple and their son watching the barn fire rage in the rain. Maroussia sits on the well, observing. One of the sons, Alexi, awakens into a dream of his father washing his mother's hair. The walls are covered in mold. The ceiling collapses, but they don't seem to notice. Maroussia sees herself in a mirror, much older.

The phone rings. Alexei answers the phone, now an adult, speaking to his aging mother, Maroussia. A poster of <i>Andrei Rublev</i> hangs on the wall. He recalls the dream he had for his mother, from 1935. When Alexei remembers his mother, she looks like his ex-wife, Natalya.

And so, the film elides time, traveling through memory and dreams into vignettes, as a poet reflects on his life during his final moments.

Tarkovsky gives us film at its most subjective, letting perspective and time keep secrets from the viewer, even when the words are spoken onscreen. Throughout the movie, his father, Aseny Tarkovsky, reads his poems, which focus on the stillness of nature and finding ourselves within it.

Actors play multiple characters — mother and ex-wife, son and self at the same age, a mysterious woman who I believe is the visage of Death — further blurring our ability to distinguish what is happening when, especially as the film progresses and memory deteriorates.

The focus on the war is probably the film's bluntest and most didactic moment. Paired with a vision of Death asking Ignat to read a journal entry about the end of czarist rule in Russia, we get the sense that the director is lamenting something lost in the transition to the rule of the CCCP.

Still, if I came away from this movie with a singular meaning, I would have missed a dozen more, available for interpretation. We are not witnessing narrative, but rather music — Tarkovsky found a way to translate the art that speaks to him most into the language of cinema.

I have seen this movie a few times, and each time I have felt like I missed something fundamental. Perhaps my attention wasn't fully there, or elements of it didn't make sense to me in my younger years. Here today, I recognize how little I knew last time and how much of life's rich pain and terrible lessons are needed to see into the mirror, darkly.
