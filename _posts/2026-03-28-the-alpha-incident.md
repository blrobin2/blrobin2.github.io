A psychedelic substance dances on petree dishes. Frank and Jim observe them in disbelief as Fielder prepares the infected animals. While the scientists have some samples, the remaining substance will be shipped by rail to Denver for safe storage. Jim reports that none of the infected animals survived the trials.

On the train, conductor Sorensen keeps an eye on the precious cargo while sidehand Hank coughs, spits, and laughs about what could go wrong. That night, after Sorensen knocks off for a bit and Hank downs a bottle or two, Hank gets curious about the cargo. He slips Sorensen's keys away and opens the crate of substance, pulling out some bottles. The train shifts, knocking the bottles out of Hank's hand, sending them shattering across the floor.

The next morning, Charlie comes into work at Moose Point station. Alvin lets him know about a train coming in that'll split up at the stop. Frank and Jim come into the lab to find one of their mice with its brain blown out of its skull. Jack stumbles in to assist with the train split. The train stops at Moose Point.

When Sorensen notices Hank's hand is cut, he checks the boxcar. Sorensen returns and calls in an Alpha 795 — the virus is in the air, and the area must be quarantined. Will these folks take to Sorensen bossing them around? What is this virus, and why is there so much secrecy around it?

Bill Rebane gets in on the 70s disaster trend, knowing enough of the tropes to keep the movie afloat, but lacking the budget to bloat the cast with recognizable faces (a common problem with 70s disaster flicks). Still, the performances are decent, even if the dialogue is so trope-heavy that it drags the movie down.

The movie has some genuine moments that work. When it lets the emotions dictate the scene more than the plot, we see some scared people wrestling with their humanity. Ralph Meeker and Carole Irene Newell are good actors when given the right material. My favorite performance is from Buck Flower, who plays Hank. He toes the line between funny and obnoxious, which is exactly what the person who caused the problem should be.

But it's renegade Jack who causes the most problems, resisting the quarantine almost immediately, and forcing Sorensen to pull a gun. For Jack, the whole thing is an infringement on his civil liberties. In his defense, Sorensen is giving them zero guidance other than "Don't move," even though he clearly knows enough to settle them down. Still, even when all the cards are on the table, he throws caution to the wind. He has a story about being in Guantanamo Bay in '62 that the movie mercifully cuts short.

The movie waits until the last 15 minutes to introduce any gore, but it's pretty gross when it finally arrives!

I would have died first because I am the sleepiest person ever to live.
