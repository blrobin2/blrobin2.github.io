<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qaTwm/detail">Asian Cinema Challenge 2023 | 48/52 | Directed by Takeshi Kitano</a></b>

Kudo asks Detective Horibe and Detective Nishi about their friendship since junior high school. Horibe brings up Nishi's wife, Miyuki, who is in the hospital after a two-year battle with leukemia. They also lost their young daughter. Horibe checks on a stakeout with Takana and Nakamura, taking Nakamura's place. Nishi and Nakamura go to the hospital so Nishi can visit his wife while Nakamura waits in the car, eager to get to a date. The doctor suggests that Nishi take Miyuki home and care for her there.

Near the stakeout, a man shoots Horibe in the stomach. He tries to climb back into the van. Nakamura grabs Nishi, and the two rush to the scene. We jump in time to Nishi walking alongside Horibe, who is now in a wheelchair. Horibe's wife left him, taking their daughter with her. Since he cannot work, he longs to be a painter but cannot afford it. Nishi also visits Tanaka's widow, giving us a one-second flash that reveals it was he who died. He also visits with Nakamura, who was also injured in the process. All of them assure Nishi that it wasn't his fault, but he cannot get the images out of his head.

Two yakuza grunts accost Nishi, reminding him that he hasn't paid the interest on his loan this month. We discover that Nishi has retired from the police force. Nishi jabs a utensil into one's eye and kicks another in the stomach, causing him to expel blood from his mouth forcibly. Nishi goes to the doctor to get his wound treated, and the doctor gives him some pills for Miyuki. He suggests that Nishi take her on a trip and talk to her as much as possible.

The film follows these characters through this season of their life.

Written, directed by, and starring Takeshi Kitano, the film blends an empathetic approach to characterization with a nihilistic worldview. Joe Hisaishi's sweeping, emotive score gives the film a much-needed lift throughout, sustaining its core emotional throughlines. This film was the first from Kitano to gain international recognition and respect in his home country of Japan.

The film has an irregular structure, doing almost everything it can to withhold information from us. I tried to structure the opening as coherently as I could, but it was so difficult without names or context. That's part of the appeal, though — the film is hellbent on eschewing the crime/revenge formula, to the point where the plot seems secondary to the experience of being with the characters. Hideo Yamamoto's stylish cinematography complements this approach, drawing juxtapositions from out-of-sequence moments.

The film seems to occupy this low-energy, reflective serenity for large swaths of its runtime, only for violence to explode, almost as if out of nowhere. Sometimes, it happens just off-screen, with only a witness's eyes to tell us where they are or a shadow to tell us what is happening. When we do see violence, it's so explosive and over-the-top, ensuring each impact has meaning. It reminds me a bit of <a href="https://boxd.it/4ljZhr"><i>A History of Violence</i></a> in that way.

Nishi's relationship with his wife is so precious. They share several laughs and wonderful moments. She seems to have no problem with Nishi's explosive violence because he'd never point it her way — if anything, it comes out in defense of her.

Early in the story, Horibe begins painting, and his pictures become a prominent aspect of the movie. We see how he combines flowers and animals, replacing their heads and eyes with the flowers he comes across. Later, he explores a more pointillist style. The paintings, in fact, are by Takeshi Kitano — another way this film reflects such a singular vision.

If you described the plot of this movie on paper, I might have dismissed it as yet another yakuza film. But this filmmaking team's eclectic approach kept me locked in and caring, not about what unfolded but by who it happened to—an unusual, but welcome experience that I found surprisingly moving.
