<blockquote><i>In what second-rate tragedy have you once again cast me in the final role?</i></blockquote>

Paula Nelson lies in her Atlantic-Cité hotel room, reminiscing about an ex named Richard—then a knock on the door. A hotel maid brings her a whiskey. Outside, two men sit in a car, watching her window. After the maid leaves, a steady knock. Paula grabs a snub-nose pistol hidden in a book. When she opens the door, Edgar Typhus attempts to burst in, but she lets him in and feels him for weapons.

Paula asks Edgar how Richard really died. Supposedly, it was heart failure, but she suspects otherwise. She loved Richard once, and now feels indebted to solve his murder. Edgar claims he wants the same thing, but he holds a secret he won't reveal. She knocks Edgar out with a shoe and drags him to the adjoining room he'd checked out.

On Edgar, she finds a newspaper with the name of Dr. Samuel Kovo, aesthetic autopsies. David, Edgar's nephew, enters, curious but unconcerned, and returns to his writing. In the bathroom, David's girlfriend, Doris, sings. After some talk, Paula dons a trench coat, lights a cigarette, and loads her pistol. She arrives at Dr. Kovo's, finding a skull wrapped in bloodied gauze.

As Paula investigates, the men following Paula make themselves known: Paul Widmark, police inspector, and Donald Siegel. Richard's death takes her deep into a mire of politics and power.

<blockquote><i>Now fiction overtakes reality. Now there's blood and mystery. Now I feel like I'm caught up in a Walt Disney movie, but with Humphrey Bogart, so it's a political movie.</i></blockquote>

Godard's take on the detective story is formally unsurprising. It plays at political noir while maintaining a meta awareness of the film's movie-ness, with Paula addressing the camera and talking to the audience. Anna Karina also seems to acknowledge that this is the final role she'll perform in a full-length film for Godard. The cuts are playful, sometimes repeated, shaking us violently to different locations, times of day — anything to break up the conventional story.

<blockquote><i>You can fool the audience, but not me.</i></blockquote>

Everyone is melancholic and reflective about the passage of time and the trickiness of language. A bar scene involves math around people's ages, the meaning of "bar," and other absurdities. This routine takes on a deeper meaning as political intrigue bleeds into the story, and phrases like "moderate," "extremism," and the political wings come into question.

<blockquote><i>Left and Right are the same: there's no changing them. The Right, because it's so cruel, is brainless. The Left, because it's sentimental.</i></blockquote>

The sound design suggests locations that visuals cannot — Paula's hotel must be near an airport, as the roar of passenger jets obscures words and fills spaces. The sound also obscures information, such as Richard's last name. Raoul Coutard's cinematography is stunning, as always, with rich blocks of color in the clothing and the neon lights.

<blockquote><i>It was once again the kind of day for taking out a camera and making a color movie.</i></blockquote>

Where the film jumps into new territory for Godard is its more overt politics. The film interrogates the nature of a police state — the way information leaks, the reasons people die, and how impossible it is to process it.

<blockquote><i>I can't tell you just how much I hate the police.</i></blockquote>

It also explores how advertising is a fascist tool, disseminating messages through reductive narratives and centering consumerism over political action. Movies themselves are a tool of advertising—a key scene takes place in a billboard factory, with endless distraction surrounding the important discussion.

<blockquote><i>Have you ever seen a communist dreamer?</i></blockquote>

And so, through Godard's formal experimentation, we receive a secondary message — that to complicate the film is to evade the reductive nature of advertising. To confound is to draw closer. It's hard not to see some parallel between this and the film not receiving US distribution until 2009, and why so many critics are vocally ambivalent about one of Godard's most political films.

<blockquote><i>— You shouldn't be worried. Fascism won't come to pass.<br>
— On the contrary, fascism must pass, and pass it will, like sailboats, miniskirts, and rock 'n' roll. We have years of struggle ahead, and many within ourselves. That's why I'm scared: scared of being weary in advance, scared of giving up the struggle.</i></blockquote>
