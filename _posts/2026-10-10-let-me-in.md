<b><a href="">Cult Movie Challenge 2019 | 49/52 | Killer Kids</a></b>

<b>Los Alamos, New Mexico<br>
1983</b>

Police cars and an ambulance race down snowy roads. In the back of the ambulance is a man with self-inflicted acid burns covering his body. At the hospital, a detective arrives to interview the man. The detective receives a call and steps out. A nurse screams. The man has jumped out the window. On the notepad, he wrote "I'm Sory Abby"

<b>Two Weeks Earlier</b>

Twelve-year-old Owen sits in the playground next to his apartment complex. He leaves some candy on the ground, hidden in the snow, and goes inside for dinner. Back in his room, Owen spots a young girl and an older man moving into the apartment next door. The girl is barefoot in the snow but doesn't seem to mind.

The next day, Owen walks to school and notices the new apartment has boarded up its windows. At school, Kenny consistently bullies Owen, whipping him with towels and giving him wedgies. After school, he buys a pocket knife and dreams of getting revenge. Back home, he meets the young girl who moved in, but she tells him she can't be his friend.

The older man breaks into a teenager's car at the grocery store and waits in the backseat. At a train crossing, the man attacks the teenager. He hangs the body in the woods, then cuts the neck, draining the blood into jugs. The film follows Owen and the unfolding relationship that develops between him and the new people next door.

This movie is a remake I wasn't excited to see, but I was curious how it compares to the masterful original. What we get, unsurprisingly, is the Hollywood-ification of a complicated movie and a remake the original director didn't approve of. For what? Just to cash in on an American audience.

That's not to say the movie is poorly made. The filmmaking craft is there, but like Owen stabbing his pocket knife into the tree, it blunts and disambiguates the themes, with characters stating their motivations and monologuing their interiority away. The original film has such a menacing aura and sense of uncertainty that this film totally misses.

The film transplants the novel and original movie to the US. In doing so, it also places the story in the middle of the Satanic panic and Ronald Reagan's assurances that America is fundamentally a good place. His mother blesses the food. The kids say the Pledge of Allegiance at school. I wish it had deeper application, but it seems only to convey the time period.

While I believe there is room to appreciate both film adaptations, this rendition does too little to justify its existence for me. Is my score fair? Probably not. But it's honest.
