<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qa26g">Cult Movie Challenge 2019 | 11/52 | Spaghetti Western</a></b>

Three men line up before a firing squad, calling for the liberation of Mexico. Bill Tate, a white American, watches — a familiar scene during the Mexican Revolution. He walks into the train station, skipping the line with a wad of cash to buy the next ticket out, to Durango. The train, in addition to carrying passengers, also contains government munitions and rurales.

After some time, the train comes to a stop, a federal army officer bound to a stake in the middle of the tracks. Lieutenant Ferreira sends commands to the rurales to save the man, but a shower of bullets flies from the hills. Over the hill comes the head guerrilla, El Chuncho. He demands that the lieutenant give over the weapons. The lieutenant makes his own effort to save the captain, but El Chuncho shoots him too, so the train drives over them both to escape.

El Chuncho, along with El Santo and his other men, returns to their horses to chase the train. Bill makes his move, taking out the train engineer and bringing the train to a stop, allowing Chuncho's men to catch up. Bill puts handcuffs on to pose as a prisoner. Chuncho is impressed that Bill took the men out, all while handcuffed. Bill claims there is a price on his head. The rest of the guerrillas kill the remaining rurales and clear out the train.

Chuncho gives Bill the nickname Niño and allows him to join the gang. The film follows Niño and the gang as they pull off heists, collecting weapons to sell to General Elías, the Zapatista revolutionary leader. What are Bill's intentions with the gang?

The film feels more mature and considered than many other spaghetti westerns of the time. This is in large part due to Franco Solinas' involvement, a Marxist writer who had also written the screenplay for The Battle of Algiers, among others. Solinas brings his political knowledge of the Mexican Revolution to lend the film a degree of levity and authority in its handling of the subject. It stands in sharp contrast to <i><a href="https://boxd.it/b1BwPV)">Duck, You Sucker</a></i>'s fatalist, conservative take on the period.

<blockquote><i>— And so, you have then decided to murder me. Why? Is there a reason? Is it only because I'm a rich man?<br>
— No, señor. It's because we are poor men. And you've done all you can to keep us that way.</i></blockquote>

Gian Maria Volonté's Chuncho is such a vibrant central character — though he initially comes across a little ridiculous, his heart comes through as he sees more. Lou Castel's Bill Tate is an ideal enigma — we don't understand his motives, but we get the sense early on that he's a piece of shit. I actually love Klaus Kinski's guerilla priest — this is the most likable I've seen him in anything.

<blockquote><i>— A man of God. How can a religious man live with these bandits?<br>
— Christ died between two thieves, didn't he? Christ was always with the poor and downtrodden. If you were a good priest, you would do the same!</i></blockquote>

The set pieces are so fun! The film does a great job of establishing the space while maintaining a fair bit of momentum. We live in the space of the guerrillas, watching their unique heists, forced to participate and to see the fallout of their decisions. The film has one of the best endings I've seen in a western.

<blockquote><i>Don't buy bread with that, hombre. Buy dynamite!</i></blockquote>
