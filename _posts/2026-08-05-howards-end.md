<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 28/52 | The Merchant Ivory Collection</a></b>

Ruth Wilcox walks the exterior of her family home, Howards End, while her husband, Henry, entertains Helen Schlegel, along with Ruth's sons, Paul and Charles, and Ruth's daughter, Evie. Paul and Helen step away, finding a private place outside to kiss. Helen writes her sister, Margaret, to tell her that she and Paul are engaged. Their aunt Juley is worried about how little they know about the Wilcoxes, and decides to go to Howards End to sort it out.

The following morning, Paul is distant to Helen, so both decide that the engagement was a mistake and call it off. They rush to send a telegram to Margaret, telling her to tell no one of the mistaken engagement. Little do they know that Aunt Juley has just arrived by train, meeting Charles and believing him to be the one engaged to Helen. After a bit of hijinks, they work it out, but Aunt Juley is there.

A few months pass. The Wilcoxes find a flat across the street from the Schlegels for a few weeks for Charles's wedding. Helen leaves a presentation, accidentally stealing Leonard Bast's umbrella. He chases her to the Schlegels' flat, where an embarrassed Helen and miffed Margaret invite him to tea. Leonard is overwhelmed and leaves, heading back home to a lower-class London and a woman named Jacky.

The film follows the threads of the Wilcoxes, Schlegels, and Basts as they shape each other's worlds.

Merchant-Ivory wanted to go bigger than their previous E.M. Forster adaptations, <a href="https://boxd.it/5SErLf"><i>Maurice</i></a> and <a href="https://boxd.it/9vxuQD"><i>A Room with a View</i></a>, filling out the periphery with more of Forster's London and the characters therein. The result feels so much more open and colorful — though it makes the film also longer, the length feels earned.

Of the Redgrave collaborations I've seen as of late, this one is far more successful. Instead of Lynn, we get Vanessa's niece, Jemma, as Evie. Vanessa does an excellent job as Ruth Wilcox — both she and Emma Thompson share several scenes as a friendship develops between Ruth and Margaret.

Ruth is quite conservative, happy to let her husband lead discussions and have a vote that she does not. Margaret's family comes from intellectuals in Germany, so she favors the arts and discussions, having her voice heard among the many. But Margaret helps highlight just how lonely a life Ruth has been leading.

My praises for the movie won't be all too different from those mentioned in other Merchant-Ivory reviews. The production design and costumes are, of course, immaculate. The performances are perfect in that space between formality and the tensions that pull at formality's coattails.

One way this film differs from the others I've seen is the focus on Leonard Bast, who is lower-class than the bourgeois Schlegels, who are already lower-class than the wealthy Wilcoxes. It's his perspective that gives us views of London previously unseen.

When things grow dire at Leonard's work as a clerk, Margaret and Helen enlist Henry to help him find a position. His conservative nature comes through when he discusses the "battle of life," wherein people experiencing poverty are poor, and it does little good to pity them. From Margaret's perspective, his so-called "battle of life" seems to make rich men richer and poor men poorer.

<blockquote><i>— I didn't think people like you existed, except in books. But books aren't real!<br>
— They're more real than anything! When people fail you, there's still music and meaning.<br>
— That's for rich people to make them feel good after their dinner.</i></blockquote>

Overall, this film's more pointed critique of the rich makes it stand a cut above the Merchant-Ivory collaborations I've seen thus far.
