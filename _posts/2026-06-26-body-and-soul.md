<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 21/52 | Pioneers of African-American Cinema</a></b>

The Right Reverend Isaiah T. Jenkins isn't who he appears to be. When the Tatesville, Georgia police arrested him, they knew him as Black Carl. But Black Carl escaped prison, hiding out as Jenkins. He visits the Autumn Leaf Social Club, swindling the proprietor out of some money. Isabelle wakes up to find her mother, Martha Jane, passed out, a wad of cash in her hand.

Sunday comes. The reverend stands at the pulpit only to see Yellow-Curley Hinds, his former cellmate, sitting in a pew. After the service, Jenkins and Hinds speak with Isabelle and Martha Jane. Hinds takes a liking to Isabelle, although her eyes are for Sylvester, Jenkins' estranged twin brother. Back home, Jenkins gets an idea for a scheme he and Hinds can run together.

Isabelle comes home with Sylvester at her arm, asking that they be allowed to marry. Martha Jane reminds Isabelle that she saved up all that money so she can buy a house when she becomes Jenkins' wife. Isabelle tells her mother that Jenkins is a drunk and a sinner, but her mother rebukes her. Jenkins arrives, asking to see Isabelle alone to speak to her about her soul.

What are Jenkins' intentions with Isabelle? What scheme is he running with Hinds? Will Isabelle be able to escape? Will Martha Jane catch on before it's too late?

The movie makes some fascinating cuts, almost like flashes of memory, bordering on subliminal in how quickly they come and go. Some of it, however, does feel out of order, but I'm willing to accept artistic license with the storytelling. I'm also willing to believe that Director Oscar Michaeux's original cut flowed together better, but that additional footage has been lost.

Michaeux sought to make the truth of the Black experience his primary focus. Here, he explores the way in which religious authority figures exploit poor Black people. In 1925, he calls out the same system that Tyler Perry would utilize decades later to advance his career. I bring up Perry also because, like Michaeaux, he is the most successful Black director of his era.

Paul Robeson has such a powerful on-screen presence. He oozes charisma as Jenkins (which later turns quite horrifying), but still holds striking space as the twin brother, Sylvester. His performance comes across quite grounded compared to the on-screen acting style of the day. Hard to believe this was his first movie role.

The movie's only major fault, as far as I'm concerned, is the twist ending — I know others are bothered by how cut up the movie is, but enough is here to hit you right in the gut.
