Nick Longhetti leads a team in fixing a utility line, then grabs a beer. Nick gets a call about another job for the crew, but he refuses, saying he has an unbreakable date tonight. At home, his kids, Tony, Angelo, and Maria, run out the door with their grandma, Martha. Mabel, Nick's wife and the kids' mother, charges out, trying to orchestrate the kid chaos. Once Martha drives off, Mabel regrets letting them go.

Back inside, Mabel preps for her date and waits for Nick to return. Meanwhile, the work truck arrives at the job they supposedly refused. Night falls. Mabel smokes and drinks. Nick relents and calls her to tell her about the job. She accepts, then hangs up, twitching. She goes to a bar and talks to a stranger named Garson as though they're old friends. Garson orders her a tall glass of Seagram's on the rocks.

Mabel and Garson leave together, with Mabel nearly passed out on his shoulder. He drags her in, begging her not to get sick, and SAs her. She wakes up the following morning to Garson getting dressed. She calls out for Nick, and Garson gets upset and confused. She takes a shower, and when she gets out, she gets angry at Garson, insisting his name is Nick. She calls out for her kids in a panic, and Garson leaves.

Nick brings his crew to the house for breakfast. A disheveled Mabel offers to make some spaghetti, forgetting who she knows and who she doesn't. Nick loses his temper with her, making everything uncomfortable for the workers. The film follows Nick and Mabel as they navigate their marriage.

<blockquote><i>I don't mind you being a lunatic. Not lunatic.</i></blockquote>

The film is one of those Hollywood miracles. John Cassavetes wrote a play for his wife and star, Gena Rowlands, about the difficulties contemporary women face, drawing on household conversations between the families for dialogue inspiration. Cassavetes himself saw it as a means of depicting the harder parts of love that movies often leave out — the sacrifices you make for love.

Facing rejections for trying to make a movie about a middle-aged woman, Cassavetes mortgaged his house and borrowed money. Peter Falk read the script and liked it so much, he invested $500K. Due to the low budget, many of the minor roles were filled by Cassavetes' own family. He got Gena Rowlands' mother to play Mabel's mother and hired his own mother to play Nick's. Rowlands did her own hair and makeup.

The performance we watch most is Gena Rowlands as Mabel, which is astonishing for its nuances and dynamics. I know some folks can't stand any performance that doesn't immediately read as grounded or nuanced, but it's far from the melodrama some folks accuse it of. It can be harrowing and hysterical. The film does not label Mabel as experiencing any specific mental illness, for better or worse.

<blockquote><i>Come on, kids, die for Mr. Jensen!</i></blockquote>

But we also watch everyone around Mabel. We see how she makes them feel, how they navigate her. Some ignore it, while others can't look away. Some take advantage, others run, and others still call for help. Despite how people read her, though, she never asks for any of the horrible things that happen to her. And, most importantly, the most explosive aggressions don't come from Mabel but from the family who can't tolerate her. We live in a reality that isn't hospitable to people like Mabel. She has to claw her way through life with the meager options life has given her.

<blockquote><i>— I don't know what you want me to do.<br>
— Just be yourself. Be yourself.</i></blockquote>

From the onset, folks seem to know something is wrong with Nick and Mabel's relationship. Nick tries to justify to Eddie why he didn't call Mabel to tell her about the job — she's unusual, she's delicate, but she's not crazy. What he doesn't see is the way his behavior contributes to and reinforces her delicate mental state. He pretends to be ambivalent about her behavior, but he smacks her around and yells at her when she doesn't conform to his expectations.

<blockquote><i>— I always understood you, and you always understood me, and that was always just how it was, and that's it.<br>
— Don't let that mind run away on you. Don't, Mabel.<br>
— Hey, don't be sad. I know you love me.<br>
— I don't know who you are.</i></blockquote>

The result is a difficult but rewarding watch. Yes, the film is a little long. Still, it's in that Cassavetes way, where he includes lots of small talk and incidental moments, chasing the complex representation of emotion that only fully expresses itself over the passage of time. It doesn't ebb and flow — it rages forward with explosive moments that make clear how much Mabel is a victim of a world that doesn't know how to communicate with her. And damn, my heart breaks for those kids.

<blockquote><i>Will you just stand up for me?</i></blockquote>
