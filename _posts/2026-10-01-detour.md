<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qa26g/detail">Cult Movie Challenge 2019 | 46/52 | Film Noir</a></b>

Al Roberts hitches a ride into Reno, Nevada. He stops in a diner for a cup of coffee, where a trucker named Joe offers him a ride North, but Al's headed East and doesn't like the small talk. Joe puts a swing song called "I Can't Believe You're in Love with Me" on the jukebox that drives Al up the wall, and he demands they turn it off. Joe and the waiters tell Al to pipe down.

<blockquote><i>Did you ever wanna forget anything? Did you ever wanna cut away a piece of your memory or blot it out? You can't, you know — no matter how hard you try.</i></blockquote>

Al used to play piano at the Break O' Dawn Club in NYC with his girlfriend, Sue Harvey, singing lead. They'd get requests to play "I Can't Believe You're in Love with Me," and Al loved it. They were happy. That is, until Sue decided that they couldn't get married until they made it. She quit her job and moved to Hollywood, leaving Al behind.

<blockquote><i>Money. You know what that is. It's the stuff you never have enough of. Little green things with George Washington's picture that men slave for, commit crimes for, die for.</i></blockquote>

After some time, Al loses his patience and decides to hitchhike across the US to Sue. He makes it to Arizona, where he gets a lift from a guy named Haskell who is headed to LA himself. After stopping for a bite, Al takes over driving and lets Haskell sleep. Rain picks up. Al pulls over to put up the top, unable to wake Haskell. He opens the passenger door, and Haskell falls out, his head striking a rock.

Haskell is dead. What is Al going to do next? It seems he'll have to take a detour on his way back to Sue.

I'm no stranger to Poverty Row or PRC. Yeah, these studios have their share of stinkers, but budget hasn't been the dividing line between their best and worst. This film goes above and beyond, using its limited resources to evoke style and mood. Yes, it only has maybe three unique sets, but the movie feels bigger.

The cinematography makes great use of the spaces, with simple yet dynamic dolly work to add a degree of elegance. For longer outdoor scenes, the movie uses fog to differentiate space. I love how the lighting shifts when Al goes into his internal monologue: the world around him darkens, and the light bounces softly on his eyes.

The movie really came together in post-production, during which PRC gave it an unprecedented nine months. The filmmakers cut the talkier parts to make for a sharper product. Leo Erdody wrote the film's original music, including a compelling theme for the unhinged femme fatale.

You'll still catch the occasional budget limit, like reversing the negative so a car drives West instead of East, and Al climbing into what looks like the driver's seat. The narration is a little overbearing, papering over a lot of seams. And, for those who care, the movie has not one likable person. I personally don't care either way.

Still, I like the story, which packs a lot into its short runtime.
