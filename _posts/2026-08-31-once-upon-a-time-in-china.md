<b><a href="https://boxd.it/qa26g/detail" rel="nofollow">Cult Movie Challenge 2019 | 33/52 | Tsui Hark</a></b>

A flotilla of Black Flag Army ships sits in the South China Sea just south of Foshan. The crew of one such ship and Wong Fei-hung, the martial arts instructor of the local militia, watch a lion dance performance. One crew member lights firecrackers. A nearby French ship mistakes the firecrackers for gunfire and fires on the ship, injuring the dancers. Wong jumps down, taking the lion head to finish the performance.

Liu complains about the many conquering nations that have signed unequal treaties with China—Britain has Hong Kong, Portugal has Macao, Russia has Heilongjiang, and now Liu is being sent to Vietnam to fight the French. This factioning has fractured Liu's army. Liu gives Wong a fan with all the unequal treaties laid out, promising to return and erase them once and for all.

The film introduces us to our cast of characters, such as Wong's apprentices at the TCM clinic he runs, Siu-kwan, his 13th aunt, and Leung Foon, an opera actor looking for a master to train him in martial arts. Leung falls for Siu-kwan and chases her around. When a foreign bullet puts a Chinese man at risk, Wong knows that Westernization is approaching fast and that something must be done.

<blockquote><i>These rolls are made from our flour. The foreigners draw boundaries here and set up forbidden areas. If we fight each other and defer to them, then soon we'll lose the flour to make rolls. </i></blockquote>

The film revives the martial arts flicks that made Golden Harvest an international name. It also serves as a launching ground for Jet Li. As folk hero Wong Fei-hung, Li is taking the mantle from Jackie Chan, who also played Wong in <a href="https://boxd.it/cx0F1d" title="Drunken Master"><i>Drunken Master</i></a>. Though Li doesn't have Chan's comedic chops, he makes up for it with stellar athleticism.

As a revival flick, the film embraces some of the genre's silliness, with Siu-kwan's camera flash bulb cooking a bird, Bucktooth So putting Leung Foon in ridiculous and heavy casts, and various slapstick moments. Not all of the set pieces have the necessary comedic timing, but they serve their function.

The film showcases the fractured China of the late 19th century during the Qing dynasty, where colonialism threatened to overwhelm more traditional ways of life. We see that as British priests walk the streets, handing out pamphlets and singing hymns, the locals play their music to drown out the tedious hymns. Many Chinese have left for America to mine for gold.

Wong represents many of those traditions, being a martial arts master and a TCM doctor. 13th Aunt is the epitome of Westernization, dressing as the British do and bringing their technology over, such as the camera. This dichotomy has been explored in many HK films, but this is perhaps the most blunt take on the theme I've seen.

I can understand why the film was so popular. It's not exactly my thing, but I can appreciate its spirit.
