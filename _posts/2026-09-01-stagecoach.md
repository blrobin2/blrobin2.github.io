<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail" rel="nofollow">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 33/52 | Western</a></b>

Lieutenant Blanchard reports to the U.S. Cavalry of Apaches destroying ranches. A Cheyenne scout with Blanchard believes that Geronimo is stirring them up. Meanwhile, Buck arrives with a stagecoach in Tonto, Arizona Territory, delivering the mining company payroll. He also lets off passengers, allowing those continuing to Lordsburg, New Mexico, to step out and stretch while Buck switches up the horses.

One traveler, Lucy Mallory, has traveled from Virginia, intent on meeting her cavalry officer husband, Richard, in Lordsburg. Lucy runs into some acquaintances, observing a notorious gambler who watches her from afar. The Law and Order League parades sex worker Dallas out of town and onto Buck's stagecoach.

Buck arrives at the U.S. Marshal's office to get his shotgun for the remainder of the journey, but the men are out hunting Ringo Kid, who has broken out of prison to avenge his father's murder by one Luke Plummer. When Buck reports seeing Plummer in Lordsburg, Marshal Curley Wilcox decides to ride shotgun for Buck.

As the stagecoach boards, we meet the alcoholic Doc Boone, the meek whiskey drummer Samuel Peacock, and performative banker Ellsworth H. Gatewood, who steals the payroll from his own bank, and Hatfield, the mysterious man who seems to know Lucy from another life. The U.S. Cavalry provides a temporary escort, and warns the passengers that they travel at their own risk, relaying Blanchard's report of Geronimo.

Oh, and, of course, there's Ringo Kidd.

Is it possible to see the world pre-Stagecoach? Can we imagine Monument Valley, Arizona as anything other than the epitome of Western locales? Can we think of John Wayne as anyone other than the colossal Western icon he became?

The Western has gone through many stages. The big-budget Western movie was once a relic of the silent movie era, relegated to B-movie status as mere Genre. John Ford made many silent Westerns, most of which were lost or incomplete, before moving into more Hollywood fare and garnering many accolades for films like <i>Arrowsmith</i> and <i>The Informer</i>.

John Wayne got bit parts throughout the silent era, working with Ford in many roles. <i>The Big Trail</i> was his first major role, but it came as Westerns were falling out of fashion and was a commercial failure, relegating Wayne to minor roles in A pictures and starring roles in B-movie Westerns.

So, when Ford wanted to revitalize the Western and bring it back to Hollywood studios, he wanted to make sure that John Wayne came along with him. This led Ford to jump through several major and independent producers before accepting some concessions, such as a lower budget and top billing to Claire Trevor.

#### Actor's Corner

- Berton Churchill was born for the banker role. A thoroughly unsympathetic depiction of a banker must've hit so hard when this came out. It certainly resonates now.
- Thomas Mitchell's alcoholic doctor actually gets some legit laughs despite playing one of the most thoroughly overplayed archetypes.
- Wayne's Ringo Kidd looks as tall as a mountain — I've seen dozens of actors emulate his relaxed, cool demeanor.
- I love how much smarter Dallas is than Ringo. My favorite Westerns have a strong woman at their center. I'm genuinely stunned at this sympathetic depiction of a sex worker — she doesn't have a redemption arc; everyone else learns to appreciate her.
- I was trying to figure out why Andy Devine's voice sounded so familiar, and it's because he voiced Friar Tuck in Disney's 1973 Robin Hood, which I watched religiously as a kid. His character, Buck, makes me laugh the most, but I don't know how much of that is my brain being programmed to laugh at his voice.
- This is, by far, the youngest and most competent I've ever seen John Carradine. I almost didn't recognize him.

The characters are quite archetypal, and brilliantly so, because it lets us meet a wide cast in a very short runtime and get a sense of each one's deal. The film knows that we need to get to the action sooner rather than later. Not that there's much action in the way of fights, but more in the dynamics played out between the actors. That said, the fights and chases come in the third act, and it's worth the wait for these moments to pack their wallop.

Of course, what hasn't stood the test of time is the film's depiction of indigenous people. This shouldn't surprise us since we continue to have problematic and unsympathetic portrayals of Native and indigenous people in cinema, with only a handful of films and television shows directed by indigenous people breaking through. If there are any serious injuries to any actors in this movie, it would be to the men who played Apaches, who take many falls to sell the film's reality.

So, for 1939, we acknowledge the ways this film doesn't hold up while appreciating the filmmaking — the epic, gorgeous cinematography, the clockwork precision of the editing, and the iconic performances that have left a lasting impression on films as we know them. From Orson Welles to Martin Scorsese, directors have looked to this movie as a textbook on filmmaking.
