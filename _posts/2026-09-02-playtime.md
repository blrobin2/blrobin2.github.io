<b><a href="https://boxd.it/q5w9E/detail" rel="nofollow">Criterion Challenge 2021 | 34/52 | Made in Italy</a></b>

The wings of some nuns' caps flap as they enter a large airport in Paris. A couple prepares to travel. A cleaner looks around, confused. A nurse carries a crying baby—a guard paces. School children enter. A photographer paces by.

The tiny scenes proliferate — dozens of little consecutive stories choreographed to perfection. It's truly impossible to watch them all. We follow a tour group into the city, full of cars and skyscrapers, all identical and nondescript.

Finally, we meet Hurot, who has an appointment at an ultramodern office building. The doorman figures out the complicated intercom, ushering Mr. Lac down a long hall where we wait the 45 seconds it takes him to get to Hurot. Lac checks the paper, then sends Hurot into a waiting room/glass enclosure.

The film moves along its own current through various buildings/set pieces in Paris, where Hurot makes it sooner or later, as well as an American tourist.

The first half of the film feels like it’s about the onslaught of modernity — the constant hum of machines and neon, the maze of visual similarity. The second half takes us to the opening of Royal Gardens, a ritzy new restaurant on the outside, but a mass of chaos beneath the surface — appearances at the cost of quality.

Without subtitles, phrases pass, and it's hard to keep up with it all. The voices join the sounds of the city, delicately guiding our eyes through the complex compositions. Still, I know I will need to watch it again just to watch a different character's trajectory that I missed in the intricate choreography.

Because of the film's near-wordless nature, I've had a few starts and stops over the years, never able to give it the full attention it deserves. Thankfully, I have someone I can watch these types of movies with, which helps keep me less distracted.

And I'm so glad I could give this movie my full attention. This is true movie magic, full of chaos and delight.

The focus on composition and matter-of-fact absurdity reminds me of <a href="https://boxd.it/4g8sdp" rel="nofollow">Roy Andersson</a>!
