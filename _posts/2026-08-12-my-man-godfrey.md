It's a classic tale of a down-and-out man, a wealthy and melodramatic woman, and the woman hiring the man as her butler to help him out. There's a lot more to it, but the story is hardly the most important part. The film is also a treat to look at. I love the wonderful animated opening credits made to look like neon lights. The costumes and sets are all pitch-perfect for the story, conveying the self-obsessed bubble in which the Bullocks live.

But what makes this movie sizzle are all the wacky performances, with William Powell's Godfrey as the servile snarker and the base reality around which all the absurdity percolates. Alice Brady is hilarious as matriarch Angelica with her off-the-wall observations. Carole Lombard's Irene is funniest when she's having an existential crisis. Both are terrific upper-class twits, but Lombard is the performance that makes this movie most memorable.

At first, the film suggests a satirical approach to wealth disparity, especially for the Great Depression at the root of why Godfrey is down-and-out in the first place. And while the film does explore this dynamic in a comedic sense, it ultimately has a favorable view of most of the Bullocks. Also, the film suddenly tidies up, cutting off its feet for fear that they might be claws, and we get a happily ever after that Godfrey didn't ask for.

I believe that Godfrey is gay, and that he appreciates Irene as much as he says, but does not love her the way she loves him. Of course, the movie ends before any of this can be rectified, but that wouldn't be a very pleasant ending, would it?

There's a reason this is the go-to reference for what constitutes a screwball comedy.
